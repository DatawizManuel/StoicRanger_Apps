## The runner from Soli
Chrysippus was born around 279 BC in Soli, a Greek city in Cilicia on the south coast of modern Turkey. As a young man he was a long-distance runner. After his inheritance was confiscated, he came to Athens and studied with Cleanthes, and for a time he also attended the Academy, where the sceptics Arcesilaus and Lacydes taught. That training in arguing both sides of a question shaped the rest of his life.

He was small, and a statue of him in the Kerameikos was said to be almost hidden behind a nearby equestrian statue, so the comic poets called him "Krysippus", the hidden one. The well-known type of his portrait shows him seated, leaning forward with one hand stretched out, as if counting arguments on his fingers.

## The second founder
Chrysippus became head of the Stoa after Cleanthes died around 230 BC. At that point the school was under heavy attack from the sceptical Academy, which argued that the Stoics could not show that any knowledge was certain. Chrysippus answered every objection and rebuilt Stoic teaching into a complete system. Ancient writers said: "If there had been no Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoa." His opponent Carneades later turned the line around: "If there had been no Chrysippus, there would have been no me."

He is said to have written more than 705 books. Almost all of them are lost; we know his views from quotations by friends and enemies, and from charred papyrus rolls found at Herculaneum.

## Logic and language
Chrysippus is one of the great logicians of the ancient world. Where Aristotle's logic dealt with terms such as "all men" and "some animals", Chrysippus worked with whole statements joined by "if", "and" and "or". He set out five basic forms of valid argument, such as "If it is day, it is light; it is day; therefore it is light", and showed how more complex arguments can be reduced to them. Modern propositional logic, developed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, rediscovered much of what he had already worked out.

## Fate and responsibility
Stoics believed that everything happens according to fate, a chain of causes running through the universe. Critics asked: if everything is fated, how can anyone be blamed or praised? Chrysippus answered with an image preserved by Cicero. Push a cylinder and it rolls; push a cone and it spins. The push starts the motion, but the shape of the object decides how it moves. Events in the world give us the push, but how we respond depends on our own character and judgment. That is where our responsibility lies.

## The passions
Chrysippus also worked out the Stoic account of emotion. Fear, grief, craving and wild delight are not forces that overwhelm reason from outside; they are judgments that something bad or good is happening, followed by an excessive impulse. Because they are judgments, they can be examined and changed. His four books On the Passions became the basis for the Stoic "therapy of the soul" that Seneca and Epictetus later practised.

## A famous death
Chrysippus died around 206 BC, aged about seventy-three. Diogenes Laertius gives two stories. In one, he drank undiluted wine at a sacrifice, grew dizzy and died a few days later. In the other, he saw a donkey eating his figs, told an old woman to give the animal some neat wine to wash them down, and laughed so hard at his own joke that he died.

Whatever the truth, the Stoicism that Romans like Cato, Seneca and Marcus Aurelius received was, in its structure, the Stoicism of Chrysippus.
