## Born a slave
Epictetus was born around AD 50 in Hierapolis, a city in Phrygia in modern Turkey. He was born into slavery, and his name simply means "acquired". His mother was a slave, and we do not know his birth name. As a boy he came to Rome as the property of Epaphroditus, a powerful freedman who served as secretary to the emperor Nero.

Epictetus was lame. One story, told by the Christian writer Origen, says his master once twisted his leg to test him. Epictetus calmly warned that the leg would break, and when it did, he said only: "Didn't I tell you it would break?" Another source says the lameness came from rheumatism. Either way, it became part of his legend.

## Student of Musonius
While still a slave, Epictetus was allowed to attend the lectures of Musonius Rufus, the leading Stoic teacher in Rome. Musonius trained him hard. Epictetus later recalled how Musonius would point out his faults so precisely that each student felt the lecture was aimed at him alone. At some point after Nero's death in 68, Epictetus was freed and began teaching philosophy himself.

## The school at Nicopolis
Around 93 or 94 the emperor Domitian expelled all philosophers from Rome. Epictetus moved to Nicopolis, a city on the west coast of Greece, and opened a school that attracted young men from across the empire, many from wealthy Roman families. The emperor Hadrian is said to have visited him.

He lived with very little: a bed, a mat and a lamp. When his iron lamp was stolen, he bought a clay one and said the thief would have to become a thief again to take the next one. Late in life he took in a child whose parents were going to abandon it, and asked a woman to help him raise it.

## The Discourses and the Handbook
Epictetus wrote nothing. A student, the historian Arrian of Nicomedia, took notes on his lectures and published them as the Discourses. Four of the original eight books survive. Arrian also produced a short summary, the Enchiridion or Handbook, meant to be kept close at hand.

The Handbook opens with the idea at the core of his teaching: "Some things are up to us and others are not." Our judgments, our desires, our choices and our actions are up to us. Our body, possessions, reputation and position are not. Freedom and peace come from caring about the first group and accepting the second. What disturbs us, he said, is not events but our judgments about them.

## Influence
Marcus Aurelius was introduced to Epictetus' notes by his teacher Junius Rusticus and quotes him throughout the Meditations. Christian monks adapted the Handbook as a guide for monastic life. In the twentieth century, the psychologist Albert Ellis cited Epictetus as an inspiration for cognitive therapy. James Stockdale, a US Navy pilot held as a prisoner of war in Hanoi for over seven years, said Epictetus' teaching kept him alive.

Epictetus died around AD 135 in Nicopolis. The portrait on his coin is an imaginary one from an engraving printed in Oxford in 1715, since no portrait made from life exists.
