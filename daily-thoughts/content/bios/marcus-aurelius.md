## A boy noticed by an emperor
Marcus Annius Verus was born in Rome on 26 April AD 121 into a wealthy family of Spanish origin. His father died when he was about three, and he was raised by his mother Domitia Lucilla and his grandfather. The emperor Hadrian took an interest in the serious, honest boy and nicknamed him Verissimus, "the most truthful", a play on his family name.

In 138, shortly before his death, Hadrian adopted Antoninus Pius as his heir, on the condition that Antoninus in turn adopt the seventeen-year-old Marcus and the young Lucius Verus. From that day Marcus was being prepared for the throne.

## Education and the turn to philosophy
Marcus had the best teachers of his age. Marcus Cornelius Fronto, the most famous orator in Rome, taught him rhetoric, and their letters survive. But in his twenties Marcus turned more and more to philosophy. His Stoic teacher Junius Rusticus gave him the notes of Epictetus' lectures, and the first book of the Meditations thanks a long list of people, from family members to philosophers, for the qualities he learned from each.

In 145 he married Faustina, the daughter of Antoninus Pius. They had at least thirteen children, many of whom died young.

## Emperor in hard times
When Antoninus died in 161, Marcus became emperor and insisted that Lucius Verus rule with him as co-emperor. Almost at once, the empire was at war with Parthia in the east. The returning army brought back a devastating epidemic, the Antonine Plague, which killed millions across the empire over the following years.

From around 166, Germanic and Sarmatian peoples, among them the Marcomanni and Quadi, broke across the Danube frontier and even reached northern Italy. In 169 Lucius Verus died, and Marcus faced the crisis alone. With the treasury empty, he auctioned off palace treasures, including gold and crystal tableware, silk garments and jewels from the imperial household, in the Forum of Trajan rather than raise taxes on the provinces. This is the event behind the one-pager on Meditations 5.20.

## Writing at the front
Marcus spent most of the 170s with his armies on the Danube, at bases such as Carnuntum and Sirmium. There, in camp, he wrote private notes to himself in Greek. Later generations called them the Meditations; the oldest title is simply "To Himself". One book ends with the note "among the Quadi, on the Granua"; another, "at Carnuntum". They were never meant for publication. They are reminders to himself: to do the work in front of him, to be patient with difficult people, to remember that everything passes, and to act for the common good.

In 175 the general Avidius Cassius, believing a false rumour that Marcus had died, declared himself emperor. The revolt collapsed within months. Marcus burned Cassius' letters unread so he would not learn which friends had been involved.

## Death and legacy
Marcus died on 17 March 180, near the Danube front, probably at Vindobona (Vienna) or Sirmium. His son Commodus succeeded him and quickly abandoned his father's wars and values.

Marcus is remembered as the last of the "Five Good Emperors", a phrase popularised by Machiavelli and later by Edward Gibbon. His bronze equestrian statue survived in Rome because people believed it showed Constantine, the first Christian emperor. The Meditations survived in a single manuscript tradition and were first printed in 1559. Today they are the most widely read work of Stoic philosophy, and the motto of Stoic Ranger, "Impedimentum fit via", comes from them.
