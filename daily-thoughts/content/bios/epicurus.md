## Born on Samos
Epicurus was born in 341 BC on the island of Samos, where his Athenian parents had settled as colonists. His father Neocles was a schoolteacher. According to one story, Epicurus turned to philosophy at fourteen, when his teacher could not explain to him what the "chaos" was from which, according to Hesiod, everything came.

He studied with Nausiphanes, a follower of Democritus' atomic theory, and at eighteen went to Athens for his military service. When the Athenian colonists were expelled from Samos in 322 BC, his family moved to Colophon on the coast of Asia Minor, and Epicurus joined them.

## The Garden
Epicurus began teaching in Mytilene on Lesbos and in Lampsacus on the Hellespont. Around 307 BC he moved to Athens and bought a house with a garden outside the city walls, where he lived and taught for the rest of his life. The school became known simply as the Garden.

Unusually for the time, the Garden welcomed women and slaves. Among its members were the courtesan and philosopher Leontion and Themista of Lampsacus, as well as Epicurus' slave Mys. The community lived simply and valued friendship above almost everything else. Over the gate, a later writer says, was the invitation: "Stranger, here you will do well to stay; here the highest good is pleasure."

## Pleasure, rightly understood
Epicurus taught that pleasure is the goal of life, which his enemies twisted into a doctrine of indulgence. In fact, he meant something close to the opposite. The highest pleasure, he argued, is the absence of pain in the body and of disturbance in the mind, ataraxia. Most of our troubles come from desires that are neither natural nor necessary, such as fame, luxury and power. He lived mostly on bread and water and once wrote to a friend: "Send me a little pot of cheese, so that I can have a feast whenever I like."

His teaching was summed up later as the "four-part cure": the gods are not to be feared; death is nothing to fear; what is good is easy to get; what is terrible is easy to endure.

## Atoms and the fear of death
Following Democritus, Epicurus held that everything is made of atoms moving in empty space. The soul too is made of atoms and scatters at death. "Death is nothing to us," he wrote, "since when we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we no longer exist." The gods exist, but live in perfect calm and take no interest in human affairs.

## Writings
Epicurus is said to have written about three hundred works. Almost all are lost. Diogenes Laertius preserved three of his letters, to Herodotus, Pythocles and Menoeceus, and his Principal Doctrines, forty key sayings. Another collection, the Vatican Sayings, was found in a manuscript in 1888. His Roman follower Lucretius set out his philosophy in the great Latin poem On the Nature of Things.

## Death
Epicurus died in 270 BC after a long, painful illness from kidney stones. In his last letter he wrote that on this happy day, which was also the last of his life, his pain was extreme, but against it he set the joy of remembering his conversations with his friends.

## Rival and teacher of the Stoics
The Stoics and Epicureans were the great rival schools of the Hellenistic world: one built life on virtue, the other on pleasure. Yet Seneca quotes Epicurus in many of his early letters to Lucilius, saying he is happy to borrow good ideas from the enemy camp, and Marcus Aurelius also cites him with respect.
