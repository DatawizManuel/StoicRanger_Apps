## A stonemason's son in Athens
Socrates was born around 470 BC in Athens, in the district of Alopeke. His father Sophroniscus was a stonemason or sculptor, and his mother Phaenarete was a midwife. Socrates later described himself as a midwife of ideas: he did not give birth to wisdom himself, but helped others bring their own thoughts into the light and test whether they were sound.

He was famously ugly by Athenian standards, with a snub nose, bulging eyes and a thick neck, and he walked barefoot in all weathers. He married Xanthippe, who became proverbial as a difficult wife, and they had three sons.

## A soldier
Socrates served as a hoplite, a heavy infantryman, in the Peloponnesian War. At the siege of Potidaea he saved the life of the young Alcibiades and then gave up the prize for bravery to him. Alcibiades later described how Socrates stood still in thought from one dawn to the next, and how he endured the bitter northern winter in his ordinary cloak. During the retreat after the defeat at Delium in 424 BC, Socrates walked away calmly, so composed that no enemy dared attack him.

## The oracle and the questions
A friend, Chaerephon, asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The answer was: no one. Socrates was puzzled, because he knew he was not wise. He went around questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen, people with a reputation for wisdom, and found that they thought they knew things they did not. He concluded that his only advantage was that he knew he did not know.

His method was to ask questions. What is courage? What is justice? What is a good life? He would take a person's answer, draw out its consequences and show that it led to contradictions, leaving both of them to search further. He said that the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being.

## Standing firm
Twice Socrates risked his life by refusing to do wrong. In 406 BC, when the Assembly wanted to try a group of generals together illegally, he alone refused to put the motion to a vote. In 404 BC the oligarchic regime of the Thirty ordered him and four others to arrest an innocent man, Leon of Salamis. The others went; Socrates simply went home.

## Trial and death
In 399 BC Socrates was charged with impiety and corrupting the young. His defence, as Plato records it in the Apology, refused to beg for mercy. He was convicted by a narrow margin. Asked to propose a penalty, he suggested free meals at public expense, as a reward for his service to the city. The jury sentenced him to death.

Friends arranged an escape, but Socrates refused, arguing in Plato's Crito that he would not break the laws of the city that had raised him. In prison he spent his last day discussing the immortality of the soul, then drank the hemlock calmly. His last words were: "Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it and do not forget."

## Legacy
Socrates wrote nothing. We know him through Plato, Xenophon and the comic poet Aristophanes. For the Stoics he was the closest thing to a real sage. Epictetus quotes him more than any other philosopher, and Zeno's path to philosophy began with a book about him.
