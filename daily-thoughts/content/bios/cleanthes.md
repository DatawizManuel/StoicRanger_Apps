## The boxer with four drachmas
Cleanthes was born around 330 BC in Assos, a town in the Troad on the north-west coast of Asia Minor. Before philosophy he was a boxer. He arrived in Athens, so the story goes, with only four drachmas in his pocket, met Zeno and decided to stay.

To pay for his studies he worked at night drawing water for the city's gardens and kneading dough for a baker, which earned him the nickname Phreantles, "the Well-Filler". Because he seemed to have no visible means of support, he was once summoned before the Areopagus council to explain how he lived. He brought the gardener he worked for as his witness. The council was so impressed that it offered him ten minae as a gift, but Zeno told him not to accept it.

## The slow student who carried the load
Cleanthes was not quick. Fellow students called him "the donkey", and he answered that he alone was strong enough to carry Zeno's burden. He compared himself to a hard writing tablet: difficult to write on, but it keeps what has been written. He was so poor that he took notes on oyster shells and the shoulder blades of oxen because he could not afford papyrus.

He studied under Zeno for nineteen years, and when Zeno died around 262 BC, Cleanthes became head of the Stoa. He led the school for more than thirty years.

## The Hymn to Zeus
Cleanthes' most famous work is the Hymn to Zeus, the longest piece of early Stoic writing that survives in full. It praises Zeus not as a bearded god on a mountain but as the reason that runs through the whole universe, steering everything and turning disorder into order. Only the bad, the hymn says, act against this order, and in doing so they harm themselves.

Another short poem of his became one of the best-known prayers in Stoic literature: "Lead me, Zeus, and you, Destiny, wherever you have ordained for me. I will follow without hesitation; and if I am unwilling and become bad, I will follow anyway." Epictetus closed his Handbook with these lines, and Seneca translated them into Latin, adding his own famous line: "The fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling."

## Teaching and physics
Cleanthes gave Stoic physics much of its vocabulary. He described the soul and the cosmos as held together by tension, a kind of inner tone, and he saw the sun as the ruling part of the universe. He wrote a commentary on Heraclitus, whose ideas about fire and the logos fed into Stoic thinking. He was also a strict moralist: he held that virtue, once acquired, cannot be lost, and that pleasure is not natural at all. Plutarch reports that he thought Aristarchus of Samos should be charged with impiety for suggesting that the earth moves around the sun.

He was less gifted at logic than at living. His own student Chrysippus is said to have told him: "Just give me the doctrines, I will find the proofs myself."

## A quiet end
Cleanthes died around 230 BC in extreme old age; sources give his age as eighty or even ninety-nine. He had a sore on his gum, and his doctors told him to fast for two days. When the sore healed and they allowed him to eat again, he refused, saying that he had already come so far along the road. He kept fasting until he died, and Chrysippus succeeded him as head of the school.

What Cleanthes gave the Stoa was continuity. He was not its most original mind, but his patience, his poverty and his loyalty to Zeno kept the school alive through its first generation.
