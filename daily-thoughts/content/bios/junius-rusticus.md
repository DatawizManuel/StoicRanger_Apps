## A family of Stoic senators
Quintus Junius Rusticus was born around AD 100 into a Roman senatorial family with a strong Stoic tradition. He was very probably the grandson of Arulenus Rusticus, a Stoic senator who was executed by the emperor Domitian in 93 for writing a book in praise of Thrasea Paetus, another Stoic who had died resisting Nero. For the Rustici, Stoicism was not an academic subject but a family inheritance, tied to the memory of men who had paid with their lives for speaking freely.

## Teacher of Marcus Aurelius
Rusticus became the most important philosophy teacher of the young Marcus Aurelius. In the first book of the Meditations, where Marcus thanks the people who shaped him, the entry for Rusticus is one of the longest. From him, Marcus writes, he learned:

that his character needed training and correction; not to be carried away by the showy rhetoric of the sophists, nor to write treatises on abstract topics or give moralising little speeches; not to parade about the house in formal dress; to write letters simply, like the one Rusticus wrote to Marcus' mother from Sinuessa; to be ready to forgive and be reconciled with those who had offended him as soon as they wanted to make peace; to read carefully and not be satisfied with a rough idea of a thing; and not to agree too quickly with people who talk a lot.

Most important of all, Rusticus lent Marcus his own copy of the notes of Epictetus' lectures. That gift set the direction of Marcus' inner life, and Epictetus is the philosopher Marcus quotes most often.

## A career in government
Rusticus was not only a teacher. He held high offices of state, serving as consul for the second time in 162, an honour Marcus gave to very few, and as urban prefect of Rome, in charge of order in the capital, from about 162 to 168. Marcus consulted him on both public and private matters.

As urban prefect, Rusticus presided over the trial of the Christian philosopher Justin Martyr and several companions around 165. The record of the trial, the Acts of Justin, preserves their exchange. Justin refused to sacrifice to the gods, and Rusticus, following the law as it stood, sentenced him to death. The episode is a reminder that a Stoic magistrate could still enforce the harsh laws of his time.

## Honours after death
Rusticus died around 170. According to a later biography of Marcus, the emperor asked the Senate to set up statues in his honour and showed him a respect he gave to few others.

## No portrait, no writings
No portrait of Junius Rusticus is known, so his coin carries the gold Stoa column. He left no writings that have survived. What we know of his teaching comes almost entirely from Marcus' grateful memory, so any one-pager for Rusticus would start from Meditations 1.7 rather than his own words.
