## From merchant to philosopher
Zeno was born around 334 BC in Citium, a harbour town on Cyprus where Greek and Phoenician culture met. His father Mnaseas was a merchant, and Zeno followed him into the trade. Ancient writers describe him as tall, thin and dark-skinned, with a slight stoop and a habit of keeping to himself.

The story of how he became a philosopher was told for centuries. In his early thirties Zeno was carrying a cargo of purple dye from Phoenicia when his ship was wrecked near Piraeus, the port of Athens. Stranded in the city, he sat down in a bookshop and listened to the bookseller read from the second book of Xenophon's memoirs of Socrates. Zeno was so taken that he asked where men like that could be found. At that moment Crates the Cynic happened to walk past, and the bookseller pointed and said: "Follow that man." Zeno later joked that he had made a prosperous voyage on the day he was shipwrecked.

## Learning from many teachers
Crates taught him the Cynic way of life: plain clothes, little food, no shame about poverty and a sharp disregard for convention. Zeno admired the toughness but was too modest for some of the Cynic stunts, and Crates is said to have teased him for it. He went on to study logic with Stilpo of Megara and attended the lectures of Polemo, head of Plato's Academy. Polemo complained that Zeno slipped in through the garden gate, stole his doctrines and dressed them up in Phoenician clothes. That was close to the truth: Zeno built something new out of everything he had heard.

## The Painted Porch
Around 300 BC Zeno began to teach in the Stoa Poikile, the Painted Porch, a public colonnade on the north side of the Athenian Agora decorated with paintings of the Battle of Marathon. Anyone could walk in and listen. His followers were first called Zenonians and later simply Stoics, after the place where they met.

Zeno divided philosophy into three parts: logic, physics and ethics. The goal of life, he taught, is "to live in agreement", which his successors expanded to "living in agreement with nature". Virtue is the only true good and vice the only true evil. Health, wealth, reputation and even life itself are "indifferent": some are reasonably preferred, but none of them can make a person good or bad. Strong passions such as fear, craving and rage are errors of judgment that a trained mind can correct. The universe is ordered by reason, the logos, and a human being is a small part of that rational whole.

His early book, the Republic, sketched a Cynic-flavoured ideal society without temples, law courts or money, bound together by love and virtue. Later Stoics found it embarrassing and tried to explain it away.

## Life in Athens
Zeno lived on bread, figs, honey and water, and his frugality became proverbial: Athenians said someone was "more self-controlled than Zeno the philosopher". He disliked crowds and would sit at the end of a couch so that at least one side of him was free. He is credited with the remark that we have two ears and one mouth so that we may listen more and speak less.

King Antigonus II Gonatas of Macedon admired him, attended his lectures and invited him to court. Zeno, by then old, declined and sent his students Persaeus and Philonides instead. Athens honoured him with a public decree, a golden crown and a tomb at public cost, stating that he had made the young men who came to him better people and had lived a life that matched his teaching.

## Death and legacy
According to Diogenes Laertius, Zeno died around 262 BC after tripping as he left the school and breaking a toe. He struck the ground with his hand, quoted a line from a tragedy, "I come, why do you call me?", and held his breath until he died.

None of his writings survive except short fragments quoted by later authors. Yet every Stoic who followed, from Cleanthes to Marcus Aurelius, built on the house he founded.
