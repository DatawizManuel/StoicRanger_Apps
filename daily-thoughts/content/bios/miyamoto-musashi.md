## A young swordsman
Miyamoto Musashi was born around 1584 in western Japan, probably in Harima or Mimasaka province. His childhood name was Bennosuke, and his family was connected to the Shinmen clan. He grew up at the end of more than a century of civil war in Japan, when skill with the sword could still decide a man's future.

By his own account in The Book of Five Rings, he fought his first duel at thirteen, defeating a samurai named Arima Kihei. At sixteen he beat a strong opponent from Tajima. He may have fought at the great Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, though which side he was on is unclear.

## The Yoshioka and Ganryū-jima
Around 1604 Musashi went to Kyoto and challenged the Yoshioka family, famous sword teachers to the Ashikaga shoguns. According to tradition he defeated the head of the school and his brother, and then a large group of Yoshioka students who tried to ambush him.

His most famous duel took place in 1612 on Funashima, a small island in the strait between Honshu and Kyushu, now known as Ganryū-jima. His opponent was Sasaki Kojirō, a celebrated swordsman known for an unusually long blade. As the story is usually told, Musashi arrived late, having carved a wooden sword from a boat's oar on the way, and killed Kojirō with a single blow. Many details were added by later storytellers, but the duel made Musashi a legend.

He wrote that he fought more than sixty duels by the age of thirty and never lost.

## Two swords and the way of strategy
Musashi developed a style of fighting with two swords at once, the long sword and the short one, which he called Niten Ichi-ryū, the "two heavens as one" school. But he insisted that strategy was more than swordsmanship. The same principles of timing, rhythm, observation and adapting to circumstances applied to a duel between two men and a battle between armies, and to any craft practised seriously.

## Artist and teacher
In later life Musashi became an accomplished artist. His ink paintings, such as Shrike on a Withered Branch and Hotei Watching a Cockfight, are treasured in Japan for their spare, decisive brushwork. He also produced calligraphy and wood carvings. He took part in the suppression of the Shimabara Rebellion in 1637 to 1638, where he was injured by a stone.

In 1640 the lord Hosokawa Tadatoshi invited him to Kumamoto on the island of Kyushu. In 1643 Musashi withdrew to a cave called Reigandō in the nearby mountains to write.

## The Book of Five Rings
The result was Go Rin no Sho, The Book of Five Rings, organised into five scrolls named Earth, Water, Fire, Wind and Void. It is a manual of strategy, but also a guide to discipline and clear perception. Its list of basic principles opens with "Do not think dishonestly" and "The Way is in training". A week before his death in 1645 he wrote the Dokkōdō, "The Way of Walking Alone", twenty-one short precepts for self-discipline, among them "Accept everything just the way it is" and "Do not regret what you have done".

Musashi has no historical link to the Stoics, but his focus on training, self-command and acceptance of what cannot be changed makes him a natural companion to them. The portrait on his coin is traditionally regarded as a self-portrait.
