## A quiet successor
Zeno of Tarsus was the fourth head of the Stoa. He was born in Tarsus in Cilicia, the same region as his teacher Chrysippus; some sources say his family came from Sidon in Phoenicia. His father was named Dioscorides. He came to Athens as a young man, studied under Chrysippus and took over the school when Chrysippus died around 206 BC.

He should not be confused with the founder, Zeno of Citium, who had died more than fifty years earlier. Ancient writers already had to keep apart several philosophers named Zeno, and Diogenes Laertius lists eight of them.

## What we know
Very little survives about Zeno of Tarsus. Diogenes Laertius says he wrote only a few books but had a great many students. In other words, he seems to have been a teacher more than an author, someone who kept the school running after the towering output of Chrysippus.

One doctrine is linked with his name. The early Stoics believed that the universe goes through endless cycles: at the end of each one, everything returns to pure fire in a great "conflagration", and then the world is reborn exactly as before. Zeno of Tarsus is reported to have suspended judgment on this teaching. He did not reject it outright, but he would not affirm it either. It is the first sign of a more cautious attitude to Stoic physics that later heads of the school, such as Diogenes of Babylon and Panaetius, carried further.

## Why there is no portrait
No statue, bust or coin with a reliable identification of Zeno of Tarsus is known. That is why his challenge coin carries the gold Stoa column instead of a face: the column stands for the school itself, which he served as head.

## No direct quotes
None of his own words have come down to us. Any line attributed to him today should be treated with suspicion. For the Daily Thoughts app this means his page will mostly serve as a link in the chain from Chrysippus to Diogenes of Babylon, rather than as the source of one-pagers.

## His place in the story
Zeno of Tarsus led the Stoa during a period when Athens was no longer a major political power but was still the intellectual capital of the Greek world. Rome was rising in the west and would soon reach into Greece itself. The school he handed on to Diogenes of Babylon was stable, respected and increasingly cautious about its boldest claims. That careful, practical Stoicism is the version that would soon travel to Rome and win over its elite.
