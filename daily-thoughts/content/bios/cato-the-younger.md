## A stern young Roman
Marcus Porcius Cato was born in 95 BC, the great-grandson of Cato the Elder, the famously severe censor. Orphaned as a child, he grew up in the house of his uncle Marcus Livius Drusus. Plutarch says he was stubborn and serious even as a boy. When the dictator Sulla's men were killing political enemies, the young Cato asked his tutor for a sword so he could free Rome from tyranny.

He studied Stoic philosophy with Antipater of Tyre and later kept the Stoic Athenodorus Cordylion in his own household. He trained himself in hardship: walking bareheaded in heat and rain, travelling on foot while his friends rode, and dressing in a plain dark toga instead of the fashionable purple.

## Integrity in office
As military tribune in Macedonia in 67 BC, he shared the work and food of his soldiers. As quaestor in 64 BC he took control of the treasury, refused to pay out false claims and made the clerks who had grown rich on fraud answer for it. He became known as the one man in Rome who could not be bribed.

In December 63 BC the Senate debated what to do with the arrested conspirators of Catiline. Julius Caesar argued for life imprisonment. Cato replied with a speech so forceful that the Senate voted for execution. The historian Sallust later contrasted the two men: Caesar won glory by giving, Cato by refusing.

## Against the strong men
For the rest of his life Cato opposed the men who were turning the Republic into a contest of personal power: Pompey, Crassus and above all Caesar. He used every legal tool, including speaking until sunset to block votes. In 58 BC his enemies sent him away to annex Cyprus. He collected the royal treasure and brought nearly 7,000 talents back to Rome, with accounts so careful that he kept two copies. Both were lost on the way, one in a shipwreck and one in a fire.

## Civil war and Utica
When civil war broke out in 49 BC, Cato sided with Pompey as the lesser danger. After Caesar's victory at Pharsalus in 48 BC, Cato led the surviving troops on a long march across the Libyan desert on foot to join the republican forces in Africa. He took command of the city of Utica.

In April 46 BC Caesar crushed the republicans at Thapsus. Cato arranged ships for everyone in Utica who wanted to escape, then spent the night reading Plato's Phaedo, the dialogue about the death of Socrates and the immortality of the soul. He stabbed himself with his sword. His doctors found him still alive and bandaged the wound, but he tore it open and died. When Caesar heard, he said: "Cato, I begrudge you your death, as you begrudged me the saving of your life."

## A symbol of freedom
Cato left no writings except one speech, but he became the Stoic hero in action. Seneca returned to him again and again as the model of a man who could not be broken. Lucan made him the moral centre of his poem on the civil war. Dante placed him as guardian of the shores of Purgatory. Joseph Addison's play Cato (1713) was a favourite of George Washington, who had it performed for his army at Valley Forge in 1778.
