## A pastor's son
Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche was born on 15 October 1844 in Röcken, a village in Prussian Saxony. His father, a Lutheran pastor, died in 1849, when Friedrich was four, and the family moved to Naumburg, where he grew up in a household of women: his mother, his sister Elisabeth, his grandmother and two aunts.

He was a brilliant student at Schulpforta, one of the best boarding schools in Germany, and went on to study theology and then classical philology at Bonn and Leipzig.

## Professor at twenty-four
His teacher Friedrich Ritschl thought so highly of him that in 1869, at only twenty-four and before he had even completed his doctorate, Nietzsche was appointed professor of classical philology at the University of Basel in Switzerland. During the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 he volunteered as a medical orderly. He caught dysentery and diphtheria while caring for wounded soldiers, and his health never fully recovered.

In these years he became a close friend of the composer Richard Wagner. His first book, The Birth of Tragedy (1872), celebrated Wagner's music as a rebirth of the Greek tragic spirit. The friendship later collapsed, and Nietzsche became one of Wagner's sharpest critics.

## The wanderer
Nietzsche suffered from severe migraines, eye problems and stomach illness. In 1879 he resigned his professorship with a small pension. For the next ten years he lived a wandering life in rented rooms, spending summers in Sils Maria in the Swiss Alps and winters in Genoa, Nice and Turin. He walked for hours each day and wrote in short bursts, often in aphorisms.

In this decade he wrote almost all of his major works: Human, All Too Human; The Gay Science, in which a madman announces that "God is dead"; Thus Spoke Zarathustra; Beyond Good and Evil; On the Genealogy of Morality; Twilight of the Idols, which contains the line "What does not kill me makes me stronger"; and Ecce Homo, his strange, proud autobiography. Few people read his books during his lifetime.

## Nietzsche and the Stoics
Nietzsche admired and mocked the Stoics in equal measure. In Beyond Good and Evil he made fun of the Stoic ideal of "living according to nature", arguing that nature is wasteful and indifferent, and that the Stoics were really imposing their own ideals on it. Yet his own central formula, amor fati, love of fate, sounds deeply Stoic: "I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things." He wanted not just to accept what happens but to love it, and to want nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity.

## Collapse and afterlife
In January 1889, in Turin, Nietzsche suffered a complete mental breakdown. A famous but unverified story says it began when he threw his arms around a horse being beaten in the street. He never recovered. He was cared for first by his mother and then by his sister Elisabeth, and died in Weimar on 25 August 1900.

Elisabeth, an ardent nationalist and antisemite, took control of his papers, edited them to suit her own views and later welcomed the Nazis, who used a distorted version of his work for their purposes. After the Second World War, scholars restored the original texts. Today Nietzsche is recognised as one of the most influential thinkers of the modern age. The photograph on his coin was taken in Naumburg in 1882 by Gustav Schultze.
