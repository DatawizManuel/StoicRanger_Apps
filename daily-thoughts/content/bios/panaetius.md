## A nobleman from Rhodes
Panaetius was born around 185 BC into a leading family of Lindos on the island of Rhodes. His education was wide. He studied in Pergamon with Crates of Mallos, the famous scholar of Homer, and then in Athens under the Stoics Diogenes of Babylon and Antipater of Tarsus.

## In Rome with Scipio
In the 140s BC Panaetius went to Rome and became a close friend of Scipio Aemilianus, the general who had destroyed Carthage in 146 BC. He belonged to the circle around Scipio that included the statesman Laelius and the Greek historian Polybius. When Scipio led an embassy to Egypt and the eastern kingdoms around 140 to 139 BC, Panaetius travelled with him.

Through these friendships, Stoicism found a home in the Roman ruling class. Panaetius showed Romans who led armies and governed provinces that Stoic ethics could be a practical guide for public life, not just a doctrine for sages.

## Head of the Stoa
When Antipater died around 129 BC, Panaetius became head of the school in Athens and led it for twenty years until his death around 109 BC. Historians see him as the start of the "Middle Stoa".

He softened several of the school's harder positions. He doubted the doctrine that the world would end in a great conflagration, rejected most forms of divination and astrology, and spoke with admiration of Plato and Aristotle. Instead of focusing on the perfect sage, who may never exist, he wrote for the person who is making progress: someone who is not yet wise but is trying to do better each day.

## Four roles
Panaetius' best-known idea is that each of us plays four roles, or personae. The first is our shared human nature as rational beings. The second is our individual nature: our talents, temperament and strengths. The third is the situation that chance has placed us in, such as wealth, family and political circumstance. The fourth is the role we choose for ourselves, such as a career or a way of life. A good life, he argued, respects all four. We should not try to imitate someone else's nature, but should act well as the particular person we are.

## On appropriate action
His most influential book was On Appropriate Action. It dealt with everyday duties: what a person owes to family, friends, country and themselves, and how to decide when these seem to clash. The book itself is lost, but Cicero used it as the model for the first two books of his own De Officiis, written in 44 BC. Through Cicero, Panaetius' practical ethics shaped the moral education of Europe for well over a thousand years.

## Students and legacy
Among his students were Posidonius of Apamea, who became the most learned Stoic of his age, and Hecato of Rhodes. Panaetius is said to have declined honorary Athenian citizenship, remarking that one city was enough for a sensible man.

No authentic portrait of Panaetius is known, so his coin carries the gold Stoa column. His words survive mostly in Cicero's Latin retelling rather than as direct quotations.
