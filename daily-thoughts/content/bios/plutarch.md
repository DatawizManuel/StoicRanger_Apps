## A man from Chaeronea
Plutarch was born around AD 46 in Chaeronea, a small town in Boeotia in central Greece, where Philip II of Macedon had once defeated the Athenians and Thebans. His family was prosperous and long established there. Plutarch loved his hometown and chose to live there most of his life, joking that he stayed so that it would not become smaller by one more citizen.

## Education and travels
Around AD 66 he studied in Athens with the Platonist philosopher Ammonius, and Platonism remained his own philosophy for life. He travelled to Egypt and several times to Rome, where he lectured and made influential friends. Through one of them, the consular Lucius Mestrius Florus, he received Roman citizenship and the name Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus.

He married Timoxena, and they had at least four sons and a daughter. When their two-year-old daughter, also called Timoxena, died while Plutarch was away, he wrote his wife a letter of consolation that survives. It is one of the most moving documents of ancient family life, gentle and practical, reminding her of the joy the child brought them and urging her not to let grief cancel it.

## Priest at Delphi
For roughly the last thirty years of his life Plutarch served as one of the two priests of Apollo at Delphi, the most famous oracle of the Greek world. He took the duty seriously, helped restore the sanctuary at a time when its importance was fading, and wrote several dialogues about the oracle and its meaning. The bust shown on his coin was found at Delphi and is traditionally identified as Plutarch, though that identification is not certain.

## The Parallel Lives
Plutarch's most famous work is the Parallel Lives, biographies of famous Greeks and Romans arranged in pairs, such as Alexander and Julius Caesar, or Demosthenes and Cicero. Twenty-three pairs survive, along with four single lives. His aim was not history for its own sake but character: he said that a small act, a word or a joke often reveals more about a person than battles in which thousands die. His Life of Cato the Younger is our main source for Cato's death at Utica.

## The Moralia
Plutarch also wrote a large collection of essays and dialogues now called the Moralia, around seventy-eight works. They include On Tranquility of Mind, On the Control of Anger, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend, On Listening, and On Moral Progress. Several essays attack the Stoics and Epicureans on points of doctrine. Even so, his practical advice on anger, calm and self-improvement often sounds very close to Stoic teaching.

## Legacy
Plutarch died some time after AD 119. In 1579 Thomas North published an English translation of the Lives, based on a French version by Jacques Amyot. Shakespeare used North's Plutarch as the source for Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra and Coriolanus, sometimes copying whole passages into verse. Montaigne called Plutarch his favourite author, and the Lives were read closely by the American founders and many later statesmen.
