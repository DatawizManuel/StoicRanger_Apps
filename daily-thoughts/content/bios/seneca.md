## From Corduba to Rome
Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born around 4 BC in Corduba, in the Roman province of Hispania, today Córdoba in Spain. His father, Seneca the Elder, was a wealthy writer on rhetoric. One of his brothers, Gallio, appears in the Acts of the Apostles as the governor before whom Paul was brought in Corinth.

Seneca was taken to Rome as a child. He suffered from poor health all his life, probably asthma or a lung disease, and spent several years recovering in Egypt with his aunt. As a young man he studied philosophy with the Stoic Attalus and with Sotion and Papirius Fabianus of the Sextii, a short-lived Roman school that combined Stoic and Pythagorean ideas. For a while he even gave up meat.

## Exile on Corsica
Seneca made his name as an orator and senator. According to later writers, the emperor Caligula was so jealous of his speaking that he nearly had him killed. In AD 41 the new emperor Claudius exiled him to Corsica, accused of adultery with Julia Livilla, the emperor's niece. He spent eight years on the island and wrote consolations to his mother Helvia and to the court official Polybius, both meditations on how to bear misfortune.

## Tutor and adviser to Nero
In AD 49 Agrippina, Claudius' new wife, recalled Seneca to tutor her son Nero. When Nero became emperor in 54 at the age of sixteen, Seneca and the praetorian prefect Burrus guided the government. The first five years of Nero's reign were later remembered as a time of good rule.

Then came compromise. Nero had his mother murdered in 59, and Seneca wrote the letter to the Senate justifying it. He also became enormously rich, with a fortune Tacitus put at 300 million sesterces, and critics called him a hypocrite who preached poverty from a palace. Seneca answered in On the Happy Life: the wise person may own wealth, as long as wealth does not own them.

## Retreat and writing
After Burrus died in 62, Seneca's position became dangerous. He asked to retire, offered Nero his fortune, and withdrew from public life. In these last years he wrote his greatest work, the 124 Letters to Lucilius, along with Natural Questions. Earlier works include On Anger, On the Shortness of Life, On Tranquility of Mind and On Providence. He also wrote tragedies, such as Medea, Thyestes and Phaedra, which later shaped Elizabethan drama.

## Death
In AD 65 a plot to kill Nero, the Pisonian conspiracy, was discovered. Seneca was probably not involved, but Nero ordered him to die. Tacitus describes the scene. Seneca told his weeping friends that he was leaving them the one thing he still had, the pattern of his life. He opened his veins, but because he was old and thin the blood flowed slowly. He took poison, which did not work, and finally was carried into a hot bath, where he died. His wife Paulina tried to die with him but was saved on Nero's orders.

## Legacy
Seneca is the most readable of the Stoics. His letters are short, practical and personal, full of everyday scenes and striking lines. Early Christian writers admired him so much that a fake correspondence between Seneca and Saint Paul circulated for centuries. Montaigne, Shakespeare's contemporaries and modern readers have all returned to him for practical advice on time, anger, grief and fear.
