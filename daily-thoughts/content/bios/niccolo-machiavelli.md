## A Florentine of modest means
Niccolò Machiavelli was born on 3 May 1469 in Florence, the son of Bernardo Machiavelli, a lawyer with a small income and a large love of books. Niccolò received a humanist education in Latin and read the Roman historians closely, above all Livy. He grew up in the Florence of Lorenzo de' Medici and saw the city's fortunes turn after Lorenzo's death in 1492.

## Servant of the Republic
In 1494 the Medici were driven out, and for four years the preacher Girolamo Savonarola dominated Florentine politics, until he was executed in 1498. Within weeks, the twenty-nine-year-old Machiavelli was appointed Second Chancellor of the Republic and secretary to the Ten of War, the committee in charge of foreign affairs and defence.

For fourteen years he worked as a diplomat and administrator. He was sent on missions to King Louis XII of France, to the Emperor Maximilian, to Pope Julius II and, most memorably, to Cesare Borgia in 1502 and 1503. He watched Borgia carve out a state in central Italy with speed, cunning and ruthlessness, and later used him as a model in The Prince. Machiavelli also persuaded Florence to raise its own militia instead of relying on mercenaries, and in 1509 that militia helped recapture Pisa.

## Fall, torture and exile
In 1512 Spanish troops restored the Medici to power and the Republic collapsed. Machiavelli lost his post. Early in 1513 his name was found on a list of suspected conspirators against the Medici. He was arrested and tortured with the strappado, being hoisted by his wrists tied behind his back and dropped. He admitted nothing. Weeks later, when Giovanni de' Medici became Pope Leo X, an amnesty set him free.

He retired to his small farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina outside Florence. In a famous letter to his friend Francesco Vettori, dated 10 December 1513, he described his days: trapping birds, arguing with woodcutters, playing cards at the inn. In the evening, he wrote, he took off his muddy clothes, put on the robes of court, and entered the company of the ancient writers, who answered him kindly. In those hours he wrote The Prince.

## The writings
The Prince, finished in 1513 and published only in 1532, after his death, is a short handbook on how to gain and keep power. It argues that a ruler must be willing to act against conventional morality when the survival of the state demands it, and that it is safer to be feared than loved, if one cannot be both. His longer Discourses on Livy praise republics and the civic virtue of ancient Rome. He also wrote The Art of War, a comedy called The Mandrake that is still performed, and the Florentine Histories, commissioned by Cardinal Giulio de' Medici.

## Fortune and skill
A theme of The Prince speaks directly to Stoic readers. Machiavelli writes that fortune is the arbiter of perhaps half our actions, but leaves the other half to us. Fortune is like a flooding river: it cannot be stopped once it rises, but in calm times a wise person builds dykes and embankments. What matters is virtù, energy, skill and readiness to act.

## Death
In 1527 the Medici were driven out again and the Republic was restored, but the new government did not trust Machiavelli because he had worked for the Medici. He died a few weeks later, on 21 June 1527, and was buried in the church of Santa Croce in Florence. The portrait on his coin is by Santi di Tito, painted later in the sixteenth century.
