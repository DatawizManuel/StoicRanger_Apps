## Successor of Diogenes
Antipater was born in Tarsus in Cilicia and studied in Athens under Diogenes of Babylon. When Diogenes died, around the middle of the second century BC, Antipater became head of the Stoa. He led it until his death around 130 BC, when his student Panaetius took over.

## The pen-shouter
Antipater's great opponent was Carneades, head of the sceptical Academy and one of the most brilliant debaters of the ancient world. Carneades attacked Stoic teaching in public lectures, and Antipater did not dare to face him in open argument. Instead he wrote book after book in reply. For this he was mocked as kalamoboas, "the pen-shouter", a man who shouted with his pen instead of his voice. Plutarch tells the story; it shows how much pressure the Stoics were under in this period.

## Refining the goal of life
Antipater worked hard on how to state the Stoic goal of life precisely. His versions run roughly: "to live continually choosing what is in accordance with nature and rejecting what is contrary to it", or "to do everything in one's power, continuously and without wavering, to obtain the things that are by nature preferred".

The point is subtle but important. The Stoic does aim at real things in the world: health, safety for family, success in a task. But what makes the life good is the skill and steadiness of the aiming, not whether the target is actually hit. Cicero later used the image of an archer: the archer does everything possible to hit the mark, yet the goal is to shoot well, not merely to strike the target. This distinction between doing your part and controlling the outcome is at the heart of later Stoic practice.

## The grain merchant of Rhodes
In the debate about the merchant who brings grain to Rhodes during a famine, knowing that other ships are coming, Antipater took the strict line against his teacher Diogenes. Because all people belong to one community, he argued, the merchant must tell the buyers everything he knows. Hiding the truth for profit is not compatible with honesty, even if no direct lie is told. Cicero, who reports the case, sides with Antipater.

## On marriage
A long fragment of Antipater's essay On Marriage survives in the anthology of Stobaeus. It argues that marriage and raising children are among the most necessary and fitting duties of a good citizen, and describes marriage as a full partnership in which husband and wife share body, soul and property. This warm view of family life was taken up by later Stoics such as Musonius Rufus.

## Why there is no portrait
No portrait of Antipater is known, so his coin shows the gold Stoa column. His works survive only in fragments, which means that there are very few direct quotes that can safely be attributed to him.
