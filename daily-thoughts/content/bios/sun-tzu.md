## A general of Wu
According to tradition, Sun Tzu, "Master Sun", was a man named Sun Wu who lived around 544 to 496 BC, at the end of China's Spring and Autumn period. He is said to have come from the state of Qi and to have served King Helü of Wu, a rising state in the lower Yangtze region, as a general.

## The palace women
The earliest account of his life comes from the historian Sima Qian, writing around 94 BC, several centuries later. In it, King Helü has read Sun Wu's thirteen chapters and asks whether he can test the theory on the palace women. Sun Wu divides 180 women into two companies, appoints the king's two favourite concubines as their captains, and explains the drill. When he beats the drum, the women only laugh. He explains again; they laugh again. Sun Wu declares that if orders are clear and not obeyed, the fault lies with the officers, and has the two captains executed, despite the king's protests. After that, the women carry out every command in perfect silence. The king, though grieving, made him a general.

Sima Qian adds that under Sun Wu's command Wu defeated the powerful state of Chu, captured its capital Ying in 506 BC, and made its name feared among the other states.

## Man or legend?
Historians debate whether Sun Wu existed as described. Older Chinese records of the period do not mention him, and the text of The Art of War, as we have it, seems to reflect warfare of the later Warring States period, the fifth and fourth centuries BC, with large conscript armies and professional generals. Many scholars think the book grew over time as a collection of teachings attached to a famous name.

In 1972 archaeologists opening a Han dynasty tomb at Yinqueshan in Shandong found bamboo strips from around 140 BC carrying a large part of the text, along with a separate work by Sun Bin, a later strategist said to be his descendant. The find showed that the book was already established in the form we know more than two thousand years ago.

## The Art of War
The Art of War is short, just thirteen chapters, written in brief, memorable lines. War, it says, is a matter of life and death for the state and must be studied with care. Its most famous ideas include:

that the best victory is to defeat the enemy without fighting at all; that all warfare is based on deception; that one should know the enemy and know oneself, and so not be in danger in a hundred battles; that speed, surprise and the shape of the terrain decide outcomes; and that a skilled commander first makes himself invincible and then waits for the enemy to become vulnerable.

## Influence
The book was studied by generals and statesmen across East Asia for centuries. It reached Europe in 1772 through a French translation by the Jesuit missionary Jean Joseph Marie Amiot. The British sinologist Lionel Giles published the classic English translation in 1910, which is now in the public domain. Today it is read by business leaders, athletes and coaches as much as by soldiers.

Like the Stoics, Sun Tzu teaches calm judgment under pressure and the importance of preparing what is in your control before acting. No portrait from his lifetime exists; the image on his coin is a modern statue of Sun Tzu in Yurihama, Japan.
