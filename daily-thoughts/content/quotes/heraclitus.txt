# Fragment B91 (Plutarch, On the E at Delphi 392b)
q: You cannot step twice into the same river.
c: This is the most famous saying attributed to Heraclitus, though it comes to us in later paraphrases. Another fragment (B12) says: "On those who step into the same rivers, different and different waters flow." The river stays the same river precisely because its water keeps changing. So do we.
p: Notice one thing today that seems the same as always but has quietly changed. Including you.

# Fragment B119 (Stobaeus 4.40.23)
q: A person's character
q: is their destiny.
c: In Greek this is just three words: ēthos anthrōpōi daimōn. The daimon was a guardian spirit that people believed shaped their fate. Heraclitus says the real force that shapes a life is a person's own character. What we become is not handed to us from outside; it grows from who we are.
p: Look at one pattern in your life. Ask what part of your character keeps producing it.

# Fragment B1 (Sextus Empiricus, Against the Logicians 1.132)
q: Though this logos holds always, people fail to understand it, both before hearing it and when they have first heard it.
q: Though all things come to pass in accordance with it, they are like people without experience.
c: These are the opening lines of Heraclitus' book. The logos is the rational order that runs through everything: the pattern behind change. Most people, he says, live as if asleep, unaware of the order around them. The Stoics took over his idea of the logos as the foundation of their whole philosophy.
e: c. 500 BC · Temple of Artemis, Ephesus | The book in the temple | Heraclitus is said to have deposited his single book in the great temple of Artemis at Ephesus, one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. He wrote it deliberately in an obscure style, some said, so that only the capable would approach it. The book is lost; we have only the fragments quoted by others.
p: Look for the pattern beneath one event that seems random today.

# Fragment B30 (Clement, Miscellanies 5.104)
q: This world, the same for all, no god or human made.
q: It always was and is and will be: an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures.
c: Heraclitus saw the world not as a fixed object but as a process, like a flame that keeps its shape while its fuel constantly changes. The Stoics took this idea and made fire the basic element of their physics.
p: Watch a flame or running water for a minute today. Remember that you, too, are a pattern in constant motion.

# Fragment B60 (Hippolytus, Refutation 9.10.4)
q: The road up and the road down
q: are one and the same.
c: Heraclitus loved to show how opposites are connected. The same path leads up or down depending on where you stand. Difficulty and ease, beginning and end, are often two views of the same thing.
p: Look at a problem today from the opposite direction. What does it look like from there?

# Fragment B101 (Plutarch, Against Colotes 1118c)
q: I searched myself.
c: In Greek just two words: edizēsamēn emeōuton. Heraclitus did not learn from teachers; he looked inside. Plutarch connected this with the Delphic command "know yourself". Self-examination, for Heraclitus, was the first step to understanding the world.
p: Spend ten minutes today in honest self-search. What do you find?

# Fragment B40 (Diogenes Laertius, Lives 9.1)
q: Learning many things does not teach understanding.
q: Otherwise it would have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, and Xenophanes and Hecataeus too.
c: Heraclitus mocked the famous learned men of his time. Collecting facts is not the same as understanding. The one who sees how things fit together is wiser than the one who knows a thousand separate things.
p: Take one thing you already know and spend time understanding it more deeply, instead of learning something new.

# Fragment B53 (Hippolytus, Refutation 9.9.4)
q: Conflict is the father of all things and king of all.
q: Some it shows as gods, others as humans; some it makes slaves, others free.
c: Heraclitus saw tension and opposition as the forces that make and reveal everything. Without struggle, nothing would show what it is. Elsewhere he says that justice itself is strife (B80). Difficulty is not a flaw in the world; it is how the world works.
p: Look at a conflict you are in today. What is it revealing about you, or the other person?

# Fragment B51 (Hippolytus, Refutation 9.9.2)
q: They do not understand how what is at odds agrees with itself:
q: it is a harmony of opposing tensions, as in the bow and the lyre.
c: A bow works because the string pulls one way and the wood the other. A lyre makes music through strings held in tension. Heraclitus saw the same in life: balance comes from opposing forces held together, not from the absence of tension.
p: Notice two pulls in your life that seem opposed. Ask how their tension might be useful.

# Fragment B88 (Plutarch, Consolation to Apollonius 106e)
q: The same thing is in us: living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old.
q: For these, changed around, are those, and those, changed around again, are these.
c: Heraclitus insists that opposites are not separate things but stages in one process. We are always becoming. Seeing this can soften our fear of change and loss.
p: Think of one change you are afraid of. Remember that you have already changed many times and remained yourself.

# Fragment B89 (Plutarch, On Superstition 166c)
q: Those who are awake share one common world.
q: But each sleeper turns away into a private world of their own.
c: Heraclitus contrasts the awake person, who sees the common order of things, with the sleeper, lost in private dreams. Marcus Aurelius echoes him: "We should not act and speak like people asleep." To be truly awake is to see clearly what is shared.
p: When you catch yourself lost in private worries today, wake up to what is actually in front of you.

# Fragment B123 (Themistius, Orations 5.69b)
q: Nature loves to hide.
c: The deeper structure of things is not on the surface. It must be searched for, with patience. This short saying has inspired scientists and philosophers for centuries.
p: Look beneath the surface of one thing today: a conversation, a habit, a natural object.

# Fragment B18 (Clement, Miscellanies 2.17)
q: If you do not expect the unexpected, you will not find it,
q: for it is hard to find and difficult to reach.
c: Heraclitus suggests that discovery requires openness. If you only look for what you already expect, you will never see anything new. The unexpected rewards those who are prepared to look for it.
p: Go into one familiar situation today expecting to be surprised.

# Fragment B110 (Stobaeus 3.1.176)
q: It is not better for people
q: to get all they want.
c: Heraclitus saw that getting everything we desire would not make us happy. Desire satisfied too easily loses its value, and some wants are better unmet.
p: Let one want go unmet today, on purpose. Notice that you are still fine.

# Fragment B85 (Plutarch, Life of Coriolanus 22)
q: It is hard to fight against anger.
q: Whatever it wants, it buys at the price of the soul.
c: The Greek word thumos covers anger, passion and strong desire. Heraclitus saw it as a costly master. Every time we give in, it takes something from our soul in payment. Plutarch quoted this line to describe the Roman general Coriolanus, whose rage destroyed him.
p: Before acting on a strong emotion today, ask what it will cost you.

# Fragment B43 (Diogenes Laertius, Lives 9.2)
q: Arrogance must be put out
q: more urgently than a house on fire.
c: For Heraclitus, insolence and arrogance (hubris) were the most dangerous of faults. A fire burns one house; arrogance can burn a whole life and a whole city. It must be stopped quickly before it spreads.
p: Notice one moment of arrogance in yourself today, and put it out at once.

# Fragment B44 (Diogenes Laertius, Lives 9.2)
q: The people must fight for their law
q: as for their city wall.
c: For a Greek city, the wall was its life: without it, the enemy walked in. Heraclitus says laws are just as important. A community without the rule of law is defenceless.
p: Defend one fair rule today, at work, at home or in public, even when it would be easier to let it go.

# Fragment B78 (Origen, Against Celsus 6.12)
q: Human nature has no insight,
q: but divine nature has.
c: Heraclitus was humble about human understanding. Our knowledge is limited and partial. Elsewhere he says that the wisest person, compared with God, looks like an ape (B83). Wisdom begins by recognising how little we know.
p: In one decision today, remind yourself that you see only part of the picture.

# Fragment B116 (Stobaeus 3.5.6)
q: It belongs to all people
q: to know themselves and to think well.
c: Despite his scorn for the crowd, Heraclitus believed that everyone has the capacity for self-knowledge and good thinking. It is not reserved for the few. The trouble is that most people do not use it.
p: Use your capacity for good thinking today on one question you usually avoid.

# Fragment B112 (Stobaeus 3.1.178)
q: Thinking well is the greatest virtue, and wisdom is to say what is true
q: and to act according to nature, listening to it.
c: Here Heraclitus combines thought, speech and action. Wisdom is not just knowing, but speaking truthfully and acting in harmony with the order of things. The Stoics would say almost exactly the same.
p: Today, make sure that what you think, what you say and what you do line up in one thing.

# Fragment B49a (Heraclitus Homericus, Homeric Questions 24)
q: We step and do not step into the same rivers;
q: we are and we are not.
c: In this version of the river saying, Heraclitus applies the idea directly to us. We are the same person from day to day, and yet we are constantly changing. Identity is not something fixed but something that flows.
p: Think about one way you have changed in the past year. Welcome it.

# Fragment B94 (Plutarch, On Exile 604a)
q: The sun will not overstep its measures.
q: If it does, the Furies, servants of Justice, will find it out.
c: Even the sun, Heraclitus says, must keep within limits. The universe is governed by measure and balance, and whatever oversteps its place will be brought back. For the Stoics, this became the idea of a natural law that applies to all things.
p: Where are you overstepping your measure today: in work, food, words? Pull back a little.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 9.3
q: Why are you surprised, you scoundrels?
q: Isn't it better to do this than to take part in politics with you?
c: Diogenes Laertius tells that Heraclitus withdrew to the temple of Artemis and played dice with children there. When the Ephesians gathered to stare, he answered them with these words. Disgusted with his city, he preferred children's games to their politics.
e: c. 500 BC · Ephesus | The exile of Hermodorus | The Ephesians banished Heraclitus' friend Hermodorus, saying that no one among them should be better than the rest. Heraclitus responded that all adult Ephesians should hang themselves and leave the city to the boys. Hermodorus is said to have gone to Rome and helped the Romans draft their earliest code of law.
p: If you are tired of a pointless contest today, step out of it without guilt.
s: Hermodorus: Heraclitus B121; Pliny, Natural History 34.21.

# Aristotle, On the Parts of Animals 1.5, 645a
q: Come in. There are gods here too.
c: Aristotle tells that visitors once came to see Heraclitus and found him warming himself by the kitchen stove. They hesitated, embarrassed to find the famous philosopher in such a humble place. He told them to come in. The divine is not only in temples but everywhere, even by the oven.
p: Find something sacred, or simply wonderful, in an ordinary place today.

# Fragment B111 (Stobaeus 3.1.177)
q: Disease makes health pleasant and good, hunger makes fullness,
q: weariness makes rest.
c: Heraclitus saw that we only understand good things through their opposites. Health is sweet because we know sickness. Rest is precious because we know tiredness. Hardships are not just obstacles; they give meaning to good things.
p: After any discomfort today, take a moment to enjoy its opposite fully.
