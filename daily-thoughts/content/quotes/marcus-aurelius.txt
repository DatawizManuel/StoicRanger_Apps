# Meditations 5.20
q: What blocks the action moves the action forward.
q: What stands in the way becomes the way.
c: Marcus wrote this note to himself, in Greek, during the long wars on the Danube frontier. The full passage says that people can block his actions, but they cannot block his intentions or his disposition, because the mind can turn whatever obstructs it into material for its own work.
c: The obstacle is not something to get past before the real task starts. Dealing with it well is the task. Patience, fairness or courage can only be practised on the things that test them.
e: AD 169 · Forum of Trajan, Rome | The palace auction | Plague had thinned the legions, Germanic tribes had crossed the Danube, and the treasury was empty. Rather than raise new taxes on the provinces, Marcus put the palace treasures up for public sale, including gold cups, his wife's silk and jewelled robes, and statues. The auction is said to have run for two months and paid for the war. The empty treasury became the way to fund it.
p: When something blocks you today, ask one question before you get annoyed: what does this let me practise right now?
s: Auction: Historia Augusta, Life of Marcus 17; Eutropius 8.13.

# Meditations 2.1
q: Say to yourself at daybreak: today I will meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, jealous and unsociable.
q: None of them can harm me, for none can drag me into what is shameful.
c: This is how Book 2 of the Meditations opens. Marcus prepares himself each morning for the difficult people he knows he will meet in council, at court and in the army. He goes on to say that they act this way because they do not know good from bad, and that they are still his kin, made for working together like hands, feet and eyelids.
c: The point is not to expect the worst out of gloom. It is to remove the surprise, so that rudeness meets a prepared mind instead of a raw nerve.
e: AD 171–173 · Carnuntum, on the Danube | Notes from the camp | Book 2 carries the heading "Written at Carnuntum", a legionary fortress east of modern Vienna. Marcus spent winters there directing the war against the Marcomanni and Quadi, hearing embassies, judging cases and dealing with generals and petitioners from dawn on. The difficult people of 2.1 were not abstractions; he met them every day.
p: Before the day starts, name the one person or situation most likely to annoy you, and decide now how you will meet it.

# Meditations 4.3
q: People seek retreats for themselves in the country, by the sea, in the mountains.
q: But nowhere can you retreat into more quiet and freedom from trouble than into your own mind.
c: Wealthy Romans dreamed of villas on the Bay of Naples. Marcus, who could have had any of them, reminds himself that the only retreat that is always available costs nothing and needs no travel. A few minutes of returning to his own principles renew him, he writes, and send him back to his work without resentment.
c: The retreat is not escape. It is a short, deliberate pause to remember what matters, taken in the middle of a busy life rather than instead of one.
p: Take two minutes today, wherever you are, to step back into your own head and remind yourself what you are actually trying to do.

# Meditations 4.7
q: Take away the opinion, and you take away the complaint "I have been harmed".
q: Take away the complaint "I have been harmed", and the harm is gone.
c: This is one of the shortest entries in the Meditations and one of the most Stoic. Marcus distinguishes between what happened and the story he tells himself about it. An insult, a setback or a loss is an event; "I have been wronged" is a judgment added on top.
c: He is not saying that nothing bad ever happens. He is saying that the part that eats at us afterwards is usually the judgment, and that part is ours to change.
p: When you catch yourself replaying a slight today, separate the plain facts from the verdict you added. Drop the verdict.

# Meditations 6.6
q: The best way to take revenge is not to become like the one who wronged you.
c: Marcus held absolute power and had every means of revenge. In the Meditations he repeatedly warns himself against answering wrong with wrong. Responding in kind, he says, only adds a second bad act to the first, and makes him what he disliked in the other person.
e: AD 175 · Syria and the Danube | The revolt of Avidius Cassius | Avidius Cassius, governor of Syria, declared himself emperor on a false report that Marcus had died. When Cassius was killed by his own officers three months later, Marcus showed no pleasure. He is said to have regretted losing the chance to pardon him, spared his family and burned his correspondence unread, so he would not have to punish the friends who had written to him.
p: If someone treats you badly today, decide in advance the one thing you will not do in reply: become like them.
s: Cassius: Cassius Dio 72.27–31; Historia Augusta, Life of Avidius Cassius.

# Meditations 5.1
q: At dawn, when you are reluctant to get up, have this thought ready: I am rising to do the work of a human being.
q: Why then complain, if I am going out to do what I was born for?
c: The emperor of Rome admits that he did not want to get out of bed. He argues with himself: was he made to lie under the blankets and keep warm? Plants, birds, ants and bees all do their part in the order of the world. Should a human being refuse to do theirs?
c: Motivation is not waiting for a feeling. It is remembering what you are for.
p: Tomorrow morning, before you check your phone, say one sentence to yourself about the work you are getting up to do.

# Meditations 2.11
q: You could leave life right now.
q: Let that decide what you do and say and think.
c: Marcus lived surrounded by death: the Antonine Plague, the war, the loss of most of his children. He did not use the thought of death to make himself gloomy, but to make himself honest. If this were the last day, which of today's petty grudges and postponements would still seem worth it?
e: AD 165–180 · Across the empire | The Antonine Plague | Soldiers returning from the Parthian war brought back an epidemic, probably smallpox, that swept the empire for fifteen years. Ancient writers report that at its height in Rome around two thousand people died in a day. Marcus himself is thought by some historians to have died of it, near the Danube front in 180.
p: Pick one thing you have been postponing that would matter if today were your last. Do it, or at least start it, today.

# Meditations 4.49
q: Be like the headland, on which the waves break continually.
q: It stands firm, and the seething water around it falls quiet.
c: Marcus goes on: "It is my bad luck that this happened to me." No, he corrects himself: "It is my good luck that, although this happened, I remain free from pain, neither crushed by the present nor afraid of the future." The same thing could have happened to anyone, but not everyone would have borne it so well.
c: The headland does not fight the waves or run from them. It simply stays what it is, and the sea calms around it.
p: When pressure comes today, don't push back and don't give way. Hold your position and let it break around you.

# Meditations 7.8
q: Do not let the future disturb you.
q: If you have to, you will meet it with the same reason that you use for the present.
c: Anxiety is mostly lived in advance. Marcus reminds himself that whatever comes, he will face it with the same tools he has now: judgment, courage, patience. He does not need to solve tomorrow's problems with today's worry.
e: AD 172 · Territory of the Quadi | The rain miracle | During a campaign north of the Danube, a Roman force was surrounded by the Quadi, cut off from water and suffering in the summer heat. A sudden storm broke, giving the soldiers water while lightning and hail struck the enemy. The Romans won. The event is carved on the Column of Marcus Aurelius in Rome, and pagans and Christians later argued over whose prayers had brought the rain.
p: Write down the one future worry that is taking up the most space. Next to it, write what you will do if it happens. Then put it away.
s: Rain miracle: Cassius Dio 72.8–10; Column of Marcus Aurelius, Rome.

# Meditations 8.47
q: If you are distressed by anything outside you, it is not the thing that disturbs you, but your own judgment about it.
q: And it is in your power to wipe that judgment out now.
c: Marcus continues: if your own character is what troubles you, who stops you from correcting it? And if you are distressed because you are not doing something that seems right, why not do it instead of being distressed? There is a decision hidden inside most distress.
e: AD 169 · Rome | The death of Annius Verus | Marcus' seven-year-old son Annius Verus died after surgery for a tumour behind his ear. According to his biography, Marcus mourned him for only five days, comforted the doctors, and then returned to public business, letting the games in honour of Jupiter go on so the public festival would not be interrupted.
p: When something weighs on you today, ask: is this the event, or my verdict on it? And is there a decision hiding inside this distress?
s: Annius Verus: Historia Augusta, Life of Marcus 21.

# Meditations 10.16
q: Stop talking about what a good person is like.
q: Be one.
c: This is perhaps the shortest instruction in the Meditations. Marcus spent his life reading and discussing philosophy, and he knew the danger: talking about virtue can feel like practising it. By the time he wrote this he had little patience with theory that did not change behaviour.
p: Choose one thing you believe a good person would do today, and do it without telling anyone.

# Meditations 6.30
q: See that you are not turned into a Caesar, that you are not dyed in the purple. It happens.
q: Keep yourself simple, good, pure, serious, unpretentious, a friend of justice, reverent, kind, affectionate and strong for your proper work.
c: Marcus knew that absolute power changes people. The phrase "dyed in the purple" refers to the imperial colour: he feared becoming the role instead of a person playing it. In the same entry he holds up his adoptive father Antoninus Pius as the model: patient, fair, steady, never in a hurry, never vain about small things.
e: AD 161 · Rome | Two emperors | When Antoninus Pius died, the Senate offered the throne to Marcus alone. He refused to accept unless his adoptive brother Lucius Verus was made co-emperor with equal rank. For the first time, Rome had two emperors at once. Marcus could have kept all the power; he chose to share it.
p: Notice one moment today when your title, role or status is doing the talking. Step out of it and speak as yourself.

# Meditations 4.17
q: Do not act as if you had ten thousand years to live.
q: Death hangs over you. While you live, while you can, be good.
c: The Stoics did not dwell on death to frighten themselves but to wake up. Time is the one resource that cannot be replaced. Marcus reminds himself that "later" is not guaranteed, so the good he intends to do should not be put off.
e: AD 169 · Altinum, northern Italy | Death of Lucius Verus | Returning from the northern front, Lucius Verus, Marcus' co-emperor and adoptive brother, suffered a stroke in the carriage they shared and died a few days later, aged thirty-nine. Overnight Marcus became sole ruler of an empire at war and in the grip of plague.
p: Is there something kind, honest or brave you have been saving for later? Do it today.

# Meditations 3.10
q: Each of us lives only in the present, this brief moment.
q: All the rest has already been lived, or is uncertain.
c: Marcus reminds himself how small the present moment is, how small the corner of the earth where he lives, and how short even the longest fame. That might sound depressing, but his conclusion is freeing: you cannot lose the past or the future, because you do not have them. You only ever have now.
p: Several times today, when your mind drifts to yesterday or tomorrow, bring it back with one word: "now".

# Meditations 7.59
q: Dig within. Within is the spring of good,
q: and it can always bubble up, if you keep digging.
c: The Stoics believed that everything needed for a good life is already present in a rational mind. It does not have to be bought, borrowed or approved by others. But it has to be worked at, like a well that silts up if left alone.
e: AD 176 · Athens | Chairs of philosophy | Returning from the East, Marcus visited Athens, was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries, and founded four state-paid professorships in philosophy, one each for the Platonists, Aristotelians, Stoics and Epicureans. Even rival schools received support: he believed people should have access to the springs of wisdom.
p: Spend ten quiet minutes today with a question that matters to you, without input from anyone else.
s: Chairs: Cassius Dio 72.31; Philostratus, Lives of the Sophists 2.2.

# Meditations 5.16
q: Your mind will take on the character of what you often think about,
q: for the soul is dyed by its thoughts.
c: Marcus uses the image of cloth soaking in dye. Whatever we return to again and again, resentment, envy, worry or gratitude, slowly colours the whole mind. He continues: dye it, then, with thoughts like these: wherever it is possible to live, it is possible to live well.
p: Notice what your mind returns to most often today. If you don't like the colour, choose a different thought to soak it in.

# Meditations 9.6
q: Your present judgment, grasping what is real;
q: your present action, done for the common good;
q: your present attitude, content with whatever comes from outside you. That is enough.
c: This short entry sums up the whole Stoic programme in three lines: see clearly, act well for others, accept what is not in your control. Marcus calls it "enough". Nothing else is required for a good life in this moment.
p: At three points today, check all three: am I seeing this clearly, acting for the good of others, and accepting what I can't change?

# Meditations 6.21
q: If anyone can show me that I am wrong in thought or deed, I will gladly change.
q: I seek the truth, and no one was ever harmed by the truth.
c: For someone with absolute power, admitting error is rare and costly. Marcus makes it a principle: being corrected is a gain, not a defeat. What does harm us, he adds, is staying in our own ignorance and self-deception.
e: AD c.176 · Rome | The emperor goes to school | According to later writers, Marcus as an older man still attended the lectures of the philosopher Sextus of Chaeronea. When someone asked where he was going, he answered that it is good even for an old man to learn, and he was going to Sextus to learn what he did not yet know.
p: Ask someone today where you might be wrong about something you care about. Listen to the answer without defending yourself.
s: Sextus: Philostratus, Lives of the Sophists 2.1.

# Meditations 6.48
q: When you want to cheer yourself up, think of the good qualities of the people around you:
q: one person's energy, another's modesty, another's generosity.
c: In Book 1 Marcus did exactly this: he listed what he had learned from each of his family members, teachers and friends. Here he recommends it as daily practice. Few things lift the mind so reliably as seeing the virtues of the people we live with.
p: Name three people you will see today and one good quality in each. Tell at least one of them.

# Meditations 12.4
q: I have often wondered how it is that everyone loves themselves more than anyone else,
q: yet values their own opinion of themselves less than the opinion of others.
c: Marcus notes the paradox: we would never let anyone read our thoughts for even a day, yet we care desperately what others think of us. If we really respected ourselves, we would care first about our own judgment of our actions.
p: Before you decide something today, ask what you would think of it, not what others will think.

# Meditations 7.69
q: The perfection of character is this:
q: to live each day as if it were your last, without frenzy, without sluggishness, without pretence.
c: Living as if each day were the last is often misread as a call to excess. Marcus means the opposite: no frantic rushing, no dull drifting, no acting a part. Just doing the day's work well and honestly, because there may not be another chance.
e: AD 180 · Near the Danube | The last days | When Marcus fell gravely ill, he called his friends and spoke to them calmly, more concerned about the empire than himself. According to his biography, when they wept he said: "Why weep for me, and not rather think about the plague and the death of so many others?" He died on 17 March 180.
p: Pick one task today and do it the way you would if you knew it was the last time you would do it.
s: Last days: Historia Augusta, Life of Marcus 28.

# Meditations 9.42
q: When you are offended by someone's shamelessness, ask yourself at once:
q: is it possible for there to be no shameless people in the world? It is not.
c: So do not demand the impossible, Marcus continues. This person is one of the shameless people who must exist in the world. The same applies to the dishonest and the faithless. Expecting the world to be without them is the real mistake.
c: He also reminds himself that nature has given us a virtue for each fault in others: gentleness against rudeness, for example.
p: When someone behaves badly today, tell yourself: of course such people exist. Then ask which virtue this situation is asking of you.

# Meditations 8.36
q: Do not let the picture of your whole life overwhelm you.
q: Do not try to take in all the troubles that may come. Ask of each present one: what is unbearable here?
c: A whole life of difficulty, imagined all at once, can crush anyone. Taken moment by moment, almost everything is bearable. Marcus reminds himself that neither past nor future weighs on him, only the present, and the present gets smaller the more closely you look at it.
p: If a big project or problem feels too heavy today, ask only: what is the next step, and can I bear that one?

# Meditations 6.13
q: When meat is in front of you, think: this is the dead body of a fish, a bird, a pig.
q: And this purple robe is sheep's wool dyed with the blood of a shellfish.
c: Marcus practised describing things in plain physical terms to strip away their glamour. Fine wine is fermented grape juice; the imperial purple is wool and shellfish dye. Seeing things as they are, he says, cuts through the stories that make us crave or fear them.
p: Pick one thing you crave or are impressed by today and describe it to yourself in the plainest possible words.

# Meditations 11.18
q: Kindness is invincible, if it is genuine and not a fake smile or an act.
q: What can the most aggressive person do to you if you remain kind to them?
c: This entry is part of a list of nine things Marcus reminds himself of when someone has offended him. Real kindness, he argues, disarms people. It is not weakness, because it does not depend on the other person's response.
p: Respond to one difficult person today with genuine kindness, and notice what it does to the conversation.
