# The Prince, chapter 25
q: I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions,
q: but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.
c: Many in Machiavelli's time believed that everything was ruled by fate or God, so there was no point in trying. Machiavelli refuses that view "not to extinguish our free will". He gives fortune about half, and claims the other half for human skill and effort. A Stoic would draw the line in a different place, but would agree that what is ours must be fully used.
e: 1513 · Sant'Andrea in Percussina | The Prince is written | Released after torture and stripped of office, Machiavelli retired to his small farm south of Florence. In a few months, in the evenings, he wrote The Prince and dedicated it to Lorenzo de' Medici, hoping to win back a post. It did not work. The book was published only in 1532, five years after his death.
p: Divide one situation today into Fortune's half and your half. Put all your effort into yours.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 25
q: I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings …
q: and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers.
c: Fortune, Machiavelli says, shows her power where courage has not prepared to resist her. You cannot stop the flood once it comes. But in calm times you can build the dykes. Preparation done in good times decides how you fare in bad ones.
p: While things are calm today, build one "dyke": a saving, a skill, a plan, a repaired relationship.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 17
q: It may be answered that one should wish to be both, but, because it is difficult to unite them in one person,
q: it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with.
c: This is the most famous and most misunderstood line in The Prince. Machiavelli does not say fear is better than love. He says both are best, and if one must choose, fear is more reliable, because love depends on others' goodwill while fear depends on the ruler. Read alongside the Stoics, it raises the question of what we build our security on.
p: Notice where you depend on others' goodwill today, and where you depend on your own steadiness.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 17
q: A prince ought to inspire fear in such a way that, if he does not win love,
q: he avoids hatred.
c: Machiavelli immediately limits his own advice. Being feared is useful; being hated is fatal. And hatred, he notes, comes above all from taking what belongs to others. Firmness without injustice can be respected; injustice cannot.
p: If you have to be firm with someone today, be firm without being unfair.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 18
q: A prince … ought to choose the fox and the lion;
q: because the lion cannot defend himself against snares and the fox cannot defend himself against wolves.
c: Machiavelli says a leader needs two natures: the cunning of the fox to see traps and the strength of the lion to frighten enemies. Relying on just one is not enough. Read more gently, it is a reminder that strength without insight, or insight without strength, will fail.
p: Ask which you need more of today: the fox's clear sight or the lion's courage.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 18
q: Every one sees what you appear to be,
q: few really know what you are.
c: Machiavelli observes that people judge by appearances and by results. Few have the chance to know us closely. For a prince, this is a lesson in managing his image. For a Stoic, it is a reminder that reputation and character are different things, and only one of them is in our hands.
p: Today, care more about what you are than what you appear to be.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 6
q: There is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success,
q: than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things.
c: Machiavelli explains why reformers struggle. Those who did well under the old system become enemies; those who might benefit from the new one are only lukewarm, because they do not yet believe in it. Anyone starting something new should expect resistance, and not be surprised by it.
p: If you are trying to change something today, expect resistance, and plan for it calmly.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 6
q: All armed prophets have conquered,
q: and the unarmed ones have been destroyed.
c: Machiavelli is thinking of Moses and Romulus on one side, and of the Florentine preacher Savonarola on the other. Ideas alone, he argues, do not hold when people stop believing; reformers need the means to carry their vision through.
e: 23 May 1498 · Piazza della Signoria, Florence | The fall of Savonarola | For four years the Dominican friar Girolamo Savonarola dominated Florence with fiery preaching, bonfires of vanities and calls for moral reform. When his support collapsed, he was arrested, tortured and hanged and burned in the main square. Weeks later, the twenty-nine-year-old Machiavelli entered the service of the Republic.
p: For one goal you care about today, ask: do I have the means to carry this through, or only the wish?
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 22
q: The first opinion which one forms of a prince, and of his understanding,
q: is by observing the men he has around him.
c: A leader is judged by the people they choose. Capable and loyal advisers show good judgment; poor ones show its absence. Seneca said something similar: choose your companions carefully, because character rubs off.
p: Look at the people you spend most time with. What do they say about your judgment?
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 23
q: There is no other way of guarding oneself from flatterers
q: except letting men understand that to tell you the truth does not offend you.
c: Courts are full of flatterers, Machiavelli observes, because people love to hear good things about themselves. The only defence is to make honesty safe. A wise leader chooses a few wise people, gives them freedom to speak the truth, and listens.
p: Tell someone today that you want their honest opinion, and react well when you get it.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 14
q: To exercise the intellect the prince should read histories, and study there the actions of illustrious men,
q: to see how they have borne themselves in war, to examine the causes of their victories and defeat, so as to avoid the latter and imitate the former.
c: Machiavelli recommends learning from the past. He mentions that Alexander imitated Achilles, Caesar imitated Alexander, and Scipio imitated Cyrus. Plutarch would have agreed: lives of great people are a school.
p: Read about one person from history today and take one lesson from them.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 3
q: They knew that war is not to be avoided,
q: but is only to be put off to the advantage of others.
c: Machiavelli praises the Romans for dealing with problems early, before they grew. Postponing a necessary conflict does not make it go away; it only gives the other side time to grow stronger. Small problems are easy to cure and hard to see; big ones are easy to see and hard to cure.
p: Address one small problem today before it becomes a big one.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 15
q: How one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live,
q: that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation.
c: This is the line that made Machiavelli notorious. He insists on looking at people as they really are, not as moralists imagine them. A Stoic would not accept his conclusion, but would agree with the first step: see the world as it is before deciding how to act in it.
p: Look honestly at one situation today as it really is, not as you think it should be.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 21
q: Irresolute princes, to avoid present dangers, generally follow the neutral path,
q: and are generally ruined.
c: Machiavelli argues that sitting on the fence in a conflict usually makes enemies on both sides. The winner will not thank you, and the loser will not protect you. Taking a clear position, even with risk, is usually wiser than hiding.
p: If you have been avoiding taking a stand on something, decide where you stand today.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 13
q: The wise prince has always avoided these arms and turned to his own;
q: and has been willing rather to lose with them than to conquer with the others.
c: Machiavelli distrusted mercenary and borrowed troops: unreliable in battle and dangerous in victory. A state should rely on its own citizens. For individuals, the lesson is to build your own strength rather than depend on borrowed help.
e: 8 June 1509 · Pisa | The militia takes Pisa | For years Florence had failed to recapture Pisa with hired troops. Machiavelli persuaded the government to raise a militia of Florentine citizens and peasants, and organised it himself. In 1509, after a long blockade, Pisa surrendered. It was the high point of his career.
p: Strengthen one ability of your own today that you usually rely on others for.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 24
q: It is a common defect in man
q: not to make any provision in the calm against the tempest.
c: Machiavelli writes about the Italian princes who lost their states. They did not lose them by bad luck, he says, but by idleness: in quiet times they never thought things could change. When bad times came, they thought of flight instead of defence.
p: Do one thing today to prepare for a storm that isn't here yet.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 24
q: That deliverance is of no avail which does not depend upon yourself;
q: those only are reliable, certain, and durable that depend on yourself and your valour.
c: This could have been written by Epictetus. Machiavelli's point is political: princes who wait to be rescued by others are lost. But the principle is universal. What depends on you is the only thing you can truly count on.
p: Identify one thing you are waiting for someone else to fix. Take one step yourself.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 21
q: Nothing makes a prince so much esteemed
q: as great enterprises and setting a fine example.
c: Machiavelli points to Ferdinand of Aragon, who rose from a minor king to the first king in Christendom through bold undertakings. Reputation is earned through action, not words, and through example more than command.
p: Set a good example today in one thing others can see.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# The Prince, chapter 9
q: A prince cannot rely upon what he observes in quiet times, when citizens have need of the state,
q: because then every one agrees with him … but in troubled times, when the state has need of its citizens, then he finds but few.
c: Loyalty is easy to promise when nothing is at stake. Machiavelli warns not to mistake fair-weather friends for real ones. True loyalty only shows in hard times, and a wise person builds relationships that will hold then.
p: Be the one who shows up for someone in their trouble today.
t: Translation: W. K. Marriott, 1908 (public domain).

# Discourses on Livy, Book I, chapter 3
q: They who lay the foundations of a State and furnish it with laws must … assume that all men are bad,
q: and will always, when they have free field, give loose to their evil inclinations.
c: Machiavelli argues that good institutions must be built for people as they are, not as we wish they were. Rules should make it hard to do harm and easy to do right. In our own lives, we can design habits and surroundings the same way, so that good choices are easier.
p: Change one thing in your surroundings today that makes a bad habit harder and a good one easier.
t: Translation: Ninian Hill Thomson, 1883 (public domain).

# Discourses on Livy, Book III, chapter 9
q: He will make fewest mistakes, and may expect to prosper most,
q: who, while following the course to which nature inclines him, finds, as I have said, his method of acting in accordance with the times in which he lives.
c: Machiavelli uses the example of Fabius Maximus, whose cautious delaying tactics saved Rome against Hannibal, but who would have failed in a different situation. Success comes from matching our nature to the moment. The hard part is that we rarely change our methods even when the times change.
p: Ask yourself today whether your usual approach fits the situation, or only fits your habits.
t: Translation: Ninian Hill Thomson, 1883 (public domain).

# Discourses on Livy, Book I, chapter 58
q: That a People is wiser and more constant than a Prince.
c: This is the title of the chapter. Against all the historians who called the crowd fickle, Machiavelli argues that a people governed by laws judges better and is more consistent than a single ruler. The author of The Prince was also a convinced republican.
p: Before a decision today, ask the people it affects what they think.
t: Translation: Ninian Hill Thomson, 1883 (public domain).

# Discourses on Livy, Book I, chapter 10
q: Titus, Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus and Marcus had no need of praetorian cohorts, or of countless legions to guard them,
q: but were defended by their own good lives, the good-will of their subjects, and the attachment of the senate.
c: Machiavelli contrasts the good emperors, including Marcus Aurelius, with tyrants like Caligula and Nero, who needed armies to protect them and were killed anyway. The safest defence for a ruler, he concludes, is to deserve the goodwill of the people.
p: Act today in a way that earns goodwill rather than needing protection.
t: Translation: Ninian Hill Thomson, 1883 (public domain).

# Discourses on Livy, Book III, chapter 31
q: A great man is constantly the same through all vicissitudes of Fortune;
q: so that although she change, now exalting, now depressing, he remains unchanged.
c: Machiavelli quotes the Roman hero Camillus: "My courage came not with my dictatorship nor went with my exile." Weak people, he says, are puffed up by success and broken by failure. Great people stay the same. It is the most Stoic page Machiavelli wrote.
p: Whatever happens today, good or bad, try to be the same person at the end of the day as at the start.
t: Translation: Ninian Hill Thomson, 1883 (public domain).

# Letter to Francesco Vettori, 10 December 1513
q: When evening comes, I return home and go into my study. At the door I take off my everyday clothes, covered in mud and dirt, and put on the robes of court.
q: Decently dressed, I enter the ancient courts of the ancients, where, received kindly by them, I feed on the food that alone is mine.
c: Machiavelli describes his exile on the farm to his friend Vettori: snaring thrushes, arguing with woodcutters, playing cards at the inn. But in the evenings he talks with the great writers of the past, asks them questions and they answer. For four hours, he says, he feels no boredom, forgets every trouble, does not fear poverty and is not frightened by death.
e: 1513 · Florence and Sant'Andrea | Prison and exile | In February 1513 Machiavelli was arrested as a suspected conspirator against the Medici and tortured with the strappado, hoisted by his bound wrists and dropped. He confessed nothing. Freed in a general amnesty when a Medici became Pope Leo X, he withdrew to his farm, where this letter and The Prince were written.
p: Tonight, give yourself one hour with a great book, with the same respect you would give a great guest.
