# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.179
q: Just give me the doctrines.
q: I will find the proofs myself.
c: Chrysippus said this to his teacher Cleanthes. He was confident in his ability to argue, and later he supplied proofs for almost the whole of Stoic teaching. It became a famous line about bold self-confidence, and about the difference between knowing what is true and being able to show it.
p: Pick one belief you hold. Can you give three good reasons for it? Find them today.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.182
q: If I had paid attention to the many,
q: I would not have become a philosopher.
c: Someone criticised Chrysippus for not going to the crowded lectures of the popular teacher Aristo. This was his answer. Following the crowd is easy; finding your own way requires some independence from what everyone else is doing.
p: Notice one thing you do today only because everyone does it. Decide for yourself.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.87
q: To live virtuously is the same as to live
q: in accordance with experience of what happens by nature.
c: Chrysippus refined the Stoic definition of the goal of life. We are parts of the whole universe, he argued, so our nature is part of its nature. Living well means understanding how things actually work and fitting our lives to that understanding.
p: Learn one thing today about how something actually works: in nature, in people, in your own habits.

# Cicero, On Fate 42–43
q: Just as a person who pushes a cylinder gives it the beginning of motion, but not the ability to roll,
q: so an impression strikes us from outside, but our assent is in our own power.
c: Cicero reports Chrysippus' famous image. Push a cylinder and it rolls; push a cone and it spins. The push starts things, but the object's own shape decides how it moves. Events give us impressions; our character decides how we respond. That is where our responsibility lies.
p: When something pushes you today, notice that the push is not the response. How will your shape roll with it?

# Galen, On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato 4.2
q: When someone walks as they choose, they can stop when they want.
q: But when someone runs, the motion carries them beyond their choice.
c: Chrysippus used this image to explain the passions. A healthy impulse is like walking: we can stop. An excessive impulse, like anger or panic, is like running downhill: once started, it carries us further than we meant. That is why we should catch it early.
p: Catch one strong emotion early today, while you are still walking, and slow down.
s: Image reported by Galen, On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato 4.2.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 9.14
q: The wise person lacks nothing, yet needs many things.
q: The fool needs nothing, for he does not know how to use anything, yet he lacks everything.
c: Seneca quotes this paradox of Chrysippus. The wise use friends, tools and resources well, but they are not destroyed by their loss. The foolish could have everything and still feel empty.
p: Use something well today, a skill, a friendship, a resource, without treating it as something you could not live without.

# Cicero, On Duties 3.42
q: Someone running a race should strive with all their strength to win,
q: but must not trip a rival or push them aside with their hand.
c: Cicero quotes this saying of Chrysippus on fair competition. It is right to seek your own advantage, Chrysippus argues, but not at the cost of wronging others. Ambition is allowed; cheating is not.
p: Compete hard today at something, but cleanly.

# Seneca, On Benefits 2.17.3
q: Giving and receiving a kindness is like a game of catch:
q: the ball must be thrown so that it can be caught, and caught so that it can be thrown back.
c: Seneca reports Chrysippus' image of the ball game. A good gift is given in a way the other person can accept, and a good recipient receives it in a way that lets the exchange continue. Generosity is a skill shared by both sides.
p: Give something today in a way that is easy to receive. And receive something today with real gratitude.
s: Image reported by Seneca, On Benefits 2.17.3; wording here is a summary of Seneca's account.

# Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 7.1
q: Nature did not create disease on purpose,
q: but it came as a by-product of the good things nature made.
c: Gellius reports Chrysippus' answer to the question of why there is suffering in a world governed by reason. The human skull, Chrysippus said, had to be made of thin bones for the sake of the brain, and therefore it can be broken. Some evils are the necessary side effects of real goods.
p: Notice a frustration today that is the flip side of something good in your life. See them as one.
s: View reported by Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 7.1; wording here is a summary.
