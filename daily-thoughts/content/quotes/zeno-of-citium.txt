# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.4
q: I made a prosperous voyage
q: when I suffered shipwreck.
c: Zeno said this about the accident that changed his life. A merchant carrying a cargo of purple dye, he was shipwrecked near Athens, wandered into a bookshop and discovered philosophy. Without the wreck there would have been no Zeno the philosopher, and no Stoa.
e: c. 312 BC · Piraeus and Athens | The shipwreck | Zeno's ship, carrying purple dye from Phoenicia, was wrecked near the port of Athens. In a bookshop he heard the second book of Xenophon's memoirs of Socrates read aloud and asked where such men could be found. The bookseller pointed at Crates the Cynic, who happened to be passing: "Follow that man." Zeno did.
p: Think of a setback that later turned out to be a turning point. Ask whether today's setback might become one too.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.23
q: We have two ears and one mouth,
q: so that we may listen more and speak less.
c: Zeno was known for his quiet manner and for short, sharp replies. He disliked long speeches and preferred to listen. This line, one of his best known, reminds us that we learn far more by listening than by talking.
p: In your next conversation, listen twice as much as you speak.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.26
q: Well-being is achieved little by little,
q: and yet it is no little thing.
c: Zeno's saying contains a play on words in Greek: "little by little" and "no little thing". Happiness is not a single event or sudden breakthrough. It is built slowly through many small good choices, each one modest, but together great.
p: Make one small good choice today and count it as part of the larger work.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.87
q: The goal is to live in agreement with nature,
q: which is to live according to virtue, for nature leads us towards virtue.
c: Zeno was the first to give this definition of the Stoic goal, in his book On Human Nature. To live "in agreement" means a life that is consistent, not divided against itself. Human nature is rational, so a life in agreement with nature is one guided by reason and virtue.
p: Where today is your life out of agreement with itself, saying one thing and doing another? Bring one small part back into line.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.23
q: A friend is another self.
c: Asked what a friend is, Zeno gave this short answer. A true friend shares your aims and values so closely that what is good for them is good for you, and your concern for them becomes as natural as concern for yourself.
p: Do something for a friend today with the same care you would give yourself.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.21
q: Your ears have slid down into your tongue.
c: Zeno said this to a young man who talked too much. It is a typically sharp, funny Zeno reply: someone who speaks constantly has stopped using their ears. Zeno's school was a place to listen and think, not to show off.
p: Notice when you are talking just to fill silence today. Let the silence stay.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.22
q: Nothing is more unbecoming than arrogance,
q: especially in the young.
c: Zeno often corrected his young students' manners. He thought arrogance was especially harmful in youth, when there is most to learn and the least experience to justify pride.
p: Ask someone younger or less experienced than you for their view today, and learn from it.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.23
q: "It was fated that I should steal."
q: "Yes, and that you should be beaten for it."
c: Zeno was beating a slave who had stolen from him. The slave, who had heard the Stoic doctrine of fate, tried to use it as an excuse. Zeno's reply shows the Stoic view: fate does not cancel responsibility. Actions have consequences, and those too are part of the chain.
p: Notice one excuse you use today: "That's just how I am." Ask what consequence it brings.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.26
q: It is better to trip with the feet
q: than with the tongue.
c: A fall with the feet hurts for a moment. A slip of the tongue, an insult, a lie or a careless remark, can do harm that lasts years. Zeno valued words chosen with care.
p: Before saying something sharp today, pause. Would it be better left unsaid?

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.24
q: Tell the king that there is someone in Athens
q: who knows how to keep silent.
c: At a dinner given for ambassadors of a king, perhaps Ptolemy, Zeno said nothing all evening. As they left, the envoys asked what they should report about him to their master. This was his answer. In a culture that admired speeches, Zeno saw silence as a sign of self-command.
e: c. 270 BC · Athens and Pella | Zeno and King Antigonus | King Antigonus II Gonatas of Macedon admired Zeno, attended his lectures when in Athens and invited him to court. Zeno, by then over eighty, declined because of his age and sent two students, Persaeus and Philonides, in his place. When Zeno died, Antigonus is said to have remarked that he had lost the audience of his life.
p: In a meeting or conversation today, choose not to speak once when you would normally jump in.

# Stobaeus, Anthology 2.7.6
q: Happiness is a good flow of life.
c: Zeno defined happiness, eudaimonia, as euroia biou, "a smooth flow of life". The image is of a river running steadily, without blockage or turbulence. A life is happy when it runs consistently, guided by reason, and not when it is filled with exciting highs.
p: Look for one place where your day keeps getting stuck. Clear it so the day can flow.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 83.9
q: No one entrusts a secret to a drunkard; but one entrusts a secret to a good person.
q: So the good person will not be a drunkard.
c: Zeno liked to make his points through short logical arguments. Seneca quotes this one about drunkenness and then criticises it as too clever. But it shows Zeno's method: think clearly, step by step, and draw conclusions about how to live.
p: Reason through one decision today in steps, as Zeno would: what follows from what?

# Cicero, Orator 113
q: Logic is like a closed fist;
q: rhetoric is like an open hand.
c: Zeno demonstrated this with gestures. Clenching his fist, he called it dialectic: tight, compact argument. Opening his hand, he called it rhetoric: the same reason, spread out and made persuasive. Both have their place; good thinking needs both.
p: Today, try to state one argument tightly, in a single sentence, and then explain it generously.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.24
q: Crates, the right way to take hold of philosophers is by the ears.
q: Persuade me, then, and drag me by them. If you use force, my body will be with you, but my mind with Stilpo.
c: Crates the Cynic tried to pull Zeno away from the lectures of Stilpo, another teacher, by grabbing his cloak. This was Zeno's reply. Minds can only be won by argument, not force. It is a principle for teachers and leaders everywhere.
p: If you want to change someone's mind today, use reasons, not pressure.

# Plutarch, On the Fortune of Alexander 329A
q: We should not live divided into cities and peoples, each with its own laws,
q: but think of all human beings as fellow citizens, with one life and one order, like a herd grazing together under a common law.
c: Plutarch summarises this vision from Zeno's lost Republic. It is one of the first statements of the idea of a world community bound by reason rather than by borders. Later Stoics, from Seneca to Marcus Aurelius, built on it with their idea of the citizen of the world.
p: Treat one stranger today as if you belonged to the same city.
