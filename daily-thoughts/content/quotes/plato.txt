# Republic 7.515c
q: Such people would believe
q: that the truth is nothing but the shadows.
c: In the allegory of the cave, Plato imagines prisoners chained since birth, facing a wall. Behind them a fire throws the shadows of objects on that wall, and the prisoners take those shadows for reality. When one of them is freed and climbs into the sunlight, he is at first blinded and then begins to see things as they are. If he goes back to tell the others, they laugh at him, and might even kill him.
p: Ask yourself what "shadows" you might be mistaking for reality today: a headline, a first impression, a rumour.

# Republic 5.473c
q: Until philosophers rule as kings, or those who are now kings and rulers genuinely philosophise,
q: there will be no end to the troubles of cities, nor, I think, of the human race.
c: Plato puts this into Socrates' mouth as the most shocking idea in the Republic. He knew how absurd it would sound, and has Socrates expect a wave of laughter. But he meant it: power without wisdom brings ruin, and wisdom without power changes nothing.
e: 367 BC · Syracuse, Sicily | The philosopher and the tyrant | After the death of Dionysius I, Plato's friend Dion invited him to Syracuse to educate the new ruler, Dionysius II, as a philosopher-king. It went badly: court intrigue grew, Dion was exiled, and Plato was kept in Syracuse almost as a prisoner. He tried again in 361 BC, with no better result.
p: If you lead anything today, a team, a family, a project, ask what a wise ruler, not a powerful one, would do.
s: Syracuse: Plato, Seventh Letter; Plutarch, Life of Dion.

# Republic 4.443d
q: The just person sets their own house in order, is master of themselves,
q: brings themselves into order and becomes their own friend.
c: For Plato, justice is not first of all about laws and courts, but about the inner order of the soul. When reason leads, courage supports it, and desire follows, a person is at peace with themselves. Only someone who is just within can act justly in the world.
p: Bring one corner of your life into order today: a drawer, an inbox, a promise. Notice how it helps the rest.

# Republic 1.347c
q: The greatest penalty for refusing to rule
q: is to be ruled by someone worse.
c: Socrates argues that good people do not want power for money or honour. Why then would they ever take on the burden? Because if they refuse, people worse than them will rule instead. Responsibility sometimes means accepting a job you would rather avoid.
p: Is there a responsibility you have been avoiding because it is unpleasant? Consider who will take it if you don't.

# Republic 2.377a
q: The beginning is the most important part of any work,
q: especially with anything young and tender.
c: Plato argues that the stories children hear shape their character for life. What we take in early becomes part of us. The same is true of any project: the first steps set its direction.
p: Start one task today with special care. Get the beginning right.

# Republic 7.536e
q: Nothing taught by force stays in the mind.
q: So do not train children by force, but by play.
c: Plato argues that learning forced on people does not last. Bodily exercises done under compulsion do the body no harm, but forced learning does not take root in the soul. Let children learn through play, and you will also discover what each is naturally suited for.
e: c. 387 BC · Athens | The founding of the Academy | Plato bought land near the grove of the hero Akademos, about a mile outside the city walls, and began to teach there. Later tradition says the entrance bore the words "Let no one ignorant of geometry enter". The school taught mathematics, astronomy and philosophy for nearly three centuries, and Aristotle studied there for twenty years.
p: Learn something today because you are curious, not because you must. Notice how much more stays.

# Laws 1.626e
q: To conquer oneself is the first and best of all victories.
q: To be defeated by oneself is the most shameful and the worst of all defeats.
c: The Laws is Plato's last and longest work, a conversation between three old men walking on Crete. At the very start the Athenian stranger suggests that the real battle is not between cities, but within each person.
p: Choose one small battle with yourself today and win it.

# Symposium 211d
q: If there is any point in life worth living, it is here:
q: in the contemplation of beauty itself.
c: In the Symposium, Socrates repeats what he learned from the wise woman Diotima. Love leads us up a ladder: from one beautiful body to all beautiful bodies, then to beautiful souls, laws, knowledge, and finally to beauty itself. Every love we feel is a step toward something higher.
e: 416 BC · Athens | Agathon's victory party | The Symposium is set at a drinking party held by the tragic poet Agathon to celebrate his first victory in the drama competition. The guests, including Aristophanes and Socrates, agree to drink moderately and take turns giving speeches in praise of love. The party ends when the drunken Alcibiades bursts in.
p: Notice something beautiful today, and let it lead your thoughts to something higher.

# Phaedrus 246a
q: Let the soul be likened to the combined power of a winged pair of horses and their charioteer.
c: Plato's image: reason is a charioteer driving two horses. One is noble and obedient; the other is wild and pulls toward pleasure. A good life is not one without desire, but one where reason holds the reins and keeps both horses moving together.
p: When a strong desire pulls at you today, picture your hands on the reins. Steer, don't let it drag you.

# Republic 3.401d
q: Rhythm and harmony sink deepest into the soul
q: and take hold of it most powerfully.
c: Plato thought music shaped character more than any other part of education. What we listen to over and over influences how we feel and think. Good music brings grace to the soul; harsh or careless music brings the opposite.
p: Choose deliberately what you listen to today, and notice how it shapes your mood.

# Laws 7.803c
q: A human being has been made as a kind of plaything of God, and this is truly the best thing about us.
q: So every man and woman should spend life playing the finest games.
c: The old Athenian in the Laws suggests that human affairs are not worth extreme seriousness. Only God is truly serious. We should live with a kind of lightness, devoting ourselves to the best activities, worship, song and dance, and the education of the young, as if they were games.
p: Do one of your duties today with a lighter heart, as if it were a game worth playing well.

# Seventh Letter 341c
q: It cannot be put into words like other subjects,
q: but after long living together with the thing itself, it is born in the soul suddenly, like a light kindled from a leaping spark.
c: In this letter, written late in life, Plato explains why he never wrote a book setting out his deepest views. Understanding of the most important things cannot be handed over in writing. It comes through long conversation and shared life, suddenly, like a spark.
p: Spend time today in real conversation with someone about something that matters, without hurrying to a conclusion.

# Theaetetus 176b
q: We should try to escape from here to there as quickly as we can.
q: And escape means becoming as like God as possible; and to become like God is to become just and holy, with wisdom.
c: Socrates explains to the geometer Theodorus why evil can never disappear completely from the world. The answer is not to run away from life, but to become more just, holy and wise within it. That is the only real escape.
p: Choose one way to be a little more just today.

# Republic 9.592b
q: Perhaps there is a pattern of it laid up in heaven for anyone who wishes to see it,
q: and, seeing it, to found a city within themselves.
c: At the end of the Republic's long description of the ideal city, Glaucon objects that such a city exists nowhere on earth. Socrates agrees, but says it does not matter. The pattern can still guide anyone who wishes to live by it, building the ideal city within their own soul.
p: Think of one ideal you hold that the world doesn't live up to. Live by it yourself today anyway.

# Laws 5.731e
q: The greatest of all evils is one that most people have from birth:
q: excessive love of oneself.
c: The Athenian stranger warns that self-love blinds us. We judge our own actions too generously and others too harshly. The person who loves themselves too much cannot see the just, the good and the beautiful clearly. True self-respect means following what is better, not simply what is ours.
p: When you judge someone today, imagine judging yourself for the same thing with the same strictness.

# Phaedrus 275a
q: This invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn it,
q: because they will not use their memory.
c: Socrates tells an Egyptian story. The god Theuth invents writing and offers it to King Thamus as a remedy for memory. The king answers that writing will do the opposite: people will rely on written signs instead of their own minds and appear wise without being so. The same debate surrounds every new technology since.
p: Remember one thing today without writing it down or looking it up.

# Republic 2.369b
q: A city comes into being because none of us is self-sufficient,
q: and each of us needs many things.
c: Plato starts his search for justice by imagining how a city begins. No one can be farmer, builder, weaver and shoemaker all at once. We need one another. Human society is born from mutual need and mutual help.
p: Thank one person today whose work you depend on but rarely notice.

# Laches 192c
q: Courage seems to be a kind of endurance of the soul.
q: But not every endurance: only endurance joined with wisdom.
c: In the Laches, Socrates and two Athenian generals try to define courage. The general Laches suggests endurance. Socrates points out that stubborn endurance in a foolish cause is not courage. True courage knows what it is enduring for.
p: Before you push through something difficult today, ask: is this worth enduring?

# Euthyphro 10a
q: Is the holy loved by the gods because it is holy,
q: or is it holy because it is loved by the gods?
c: Socrates meets the confident priest Euthyphro on the way to court, where Euthyphro is about to prosecute his own father. He asks him what holiness is. This famous question asks whether things are good because an authority says so, or whether the authority approves them because they are good.
p: Ask of one rule you follow today: is it right because it is the rule, or is it the rule because it is right?

# Republic 1.329c
q: Most gladly have I escaped from it,
q: as if from a raging and savage master.
c: Old Cephalus quotes the poet Sophocles, who was asked whether he still enjoyed the pleasures of love. Sophocles answered that he was glad to be free of them. Cephalus adds that old age brings great peace and freedom from such passions, for those with a calm and contented character.
p: Notice one craving that does not have the hold on you it used to. Be grateful for the freedom.

# Symposium 215b
q: He is just like those statues of Silenus in the sculptors' shops:
q: when you open them up, they have images of the gods inside.
c: The drunken Alcibiades compares Socrates to small statues of the satyr Silenus, ugly on the outside, which opened up to reveal figures of gods. Socrates looked comical, Alcibiades says, but inside he was full of wisdom and beauty.
p: Look past someone's appearance today. What might be inside?

# Republic 10.617e
q: Responsibility lies with the one who chooses.
q: God is not to blame.
c: At the end of the Republic, Plato tells the myth of Er, a soldier who returned from the dead. He saw souls choosing their next lives from a set of lots. Some chose wisely, others greedily, and later regretted it. This is the message spoken to them before they choose.
p: When you make a choice today, own it fully. No blaming fate, others or circumstances.

# Seventh Letter 324d
q: I saw that these men in a short time made the former government look like a golden age.
c: In the Seventh Letter, Plato explains why he turned away from politics as a young man. His own relatives were among the Thirty Tyrants who seized Athens in 404 BC. Their reign of terror, and then the execution of Socrates by the restored democracy, convinced him that no city could be well governed until philosophy and power were joined.
e: 404–403 BC · Athens | The Thirty Tyrants | After Athens lost the Peloponnesian War, a pro-Spartan oligarchy of thirty men took power, among them Plato's cousin Critias and his uncle Charmides. They executed around 1,500 people and seized property before being overthrown within a year. Plato, then in his early twenties, watched it all.
p: Look at a situation you once thought was bad. Did what replaced it turn out better or worse? What does that teach?

# Republic 2.360c
q: No one is just willingly, but only under compulsion.
q: Whoever could do wrong without being seen would do it.
c: Glaucon, Plato's brother, challenges Socrates with the story of the ring of Gyges. A shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible, and uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and take the throne. Wouldn't anyone do the same? The rest of the Republic is Plato's answer: justice is worth having for its own sake, even if no one ever sees it.
p: Do one right thing today that no one will ever know about.

# Meno 80a
q: You are like the flat torpedo fish of the sea:
q: whoever touches it is numbed, and you have done the same to me.
c: Meno, a confident young aristocrat, complains that Socrates' questions have left him unable to say what virtue is, though he had given many speeches on it before. Socrates answers that he numbs others only because he is numb himself. Confusion is uncomfortable, but it is the beginning of real inquiry.
p: When something you thought you understood stops making sense today, stay with the confusion for a while.
