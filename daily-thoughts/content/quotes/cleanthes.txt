# Epictetus, Handbook 53
q: Lead me, Zeus, and you, Destiny, wherever you have assigned me.
q: I will follow without hesitation. And if I become bad and unwilling, I will follow all the same.
c: These lines by Cleanthes became one of the best-known prayers of the ancient world. Epictetus ended his Handbook with them, and Seneca translated them into Latin. They express the Stoic attitude to fate: we can follow willingly, or be dragged, but we will go either way.
p: Name one thing today that you cannot change. Say to yourself: "I will follow without hesitation."

# Hymn to Zeus, lines 1–5
q: Most glorious of the immortals, called by many names, ever all-powerful,
q: Zeus, origin of nature, steering all things with law: hail to you.
c: Cleanthes' Hymn to Zeus is the longest piece of early Stoic writing to survive complete. It addresses Zeus not as a mythical figure but as the reason that orders the universe. For Cleanthes, praising God and understanding the rational order of nature were the same activity.
p: Take a moment today to notice something ordered and beautiful in nature, and let it put your worries in proportion.

# Hymn to Zeus, lines 15–17
q: Nothing happens on earth without you, nor in the heavens, nor in the sea,
q: except what bad people do in their folly.
c: The hymn faces the problem of evil directly. Cleanthes believed that the universe is ordered by reason, but also that people can act against it through ignorance. He continues: "But you know how to make the crooked straight and to order the disorderly." Even wrongdoing is woven back into the whole.
p: When you see something go wrong today, ask: what could be made straight from this?

# Hymn to Zeus, lines 22–25
q: Unhappy people, always longing for the possession of good things,
q: they neither see nor hear God's universal law. If they obeyed it with understanding, they would have a good life.
c: Cleanthes describes the people who chase reputation, money or pleasure and never find peace. The good life is available, but they miss it because they are looking in the wrong direction. Happiness comes from understanding and following the order of things, not from possessing more.
p: Notice one thing you are chasing today. Ask whether it is the good itself, or something you think will bring it.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.168
q: I don't only draw water. I dig the ground, I water gardens,
q: and I do everything for the sake of philosophy.
c: Cleanthes worked through the nights to pay for his studies. He reportedly said this to people who wondered how he made a living. Philosophy, for him, was worth any amount of hard, humble work.
e: c. 280 BC · Athens | Before the Areopagus | Because Cleanthes was always at Zeno's lectures but seemed to have no income, he was summoned before the Areopagus, the city's ancient council, to explain how he lived. He brought the gardener for whom he drew water at night and the woman for whom he kneaded dough as witnesses. The councillors were so impressed that they voted him a gift of ten minae; Zeno would not let him accept it.
p: Do one humble task today well and without complaint, as part of something bigger you care about.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.171
q: To an old man
q: who has grey hair but no sense.
c: Someone once overheard Cleanthes scolding himself and asked who he was talking to. This was his answer. It is a funny line, but it reflects a serious practice: Stoics examined and corrected themselves constantly, even into old age.
p: Have an honest word with yourself today about one thing you know you should do better.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.171
q: That is why I make so few mistakes.
c: When someone mocked Cleanthes for being timid and overly cautious, he answered in this way. What others saw as weakness, he saw as a source of good judgment. Caution, used well, is not cowardice but care.
p: Before one decision today, take an extra moment of caution. See if it saves you a mistake.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.170
q: Yes, I am a donkey,
q: but I alone am strong enough to carry Zeno's load.
c: Fellow students called Cleanthes "the donkey" because he was slow to learn. He turned the insult around. He was not quick, but he was strong and patient, and his steady devotion made him Zeno's successor as head of the school.
p: If someone mocks you today, look for the strength hidden in what they call weakness.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.37
q: I am like a hard writing tablet:
q: difficult to write on, but I keep what is written.
c: Cleanthes compared himself to a stiff wax tablet. He learned slowly, but what he learned stayed with him. Quick minds often forget quickly; patient learners build lasting understanding.
p: Spend extra time today truly understanding one thing, rather than skimming many.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 108.10
q: As breath sounds clearer when forced through a narrow trumpet,
q: so the strict form of verse makes our thought sharper.
c: Seneca reports this image from Cleanthes, who wrote some of his teaching in poetry. Compressed into a few words, an idea strikes harder and stays longer. That is why Stoics loved short sayings and maxims.
p: Write one principle you live by in no more than ten words.

# Stobaeus, Anthology 2.7.6
q: The goal is to live in agreement with nature.
c: Zeno said the goal of life was "to live in agreement". Cleanthes, according to Stobaeus, added the words "with nature", making explicit what that agreement is with: the rational order of the universe and our own rational nature. It became the classic Stoic formula.
p: Ask yourself today: is this action in agreement with what I really am, a reasoning and social being?

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 7.176
q: I have already come so far along the road.
c: In old age Cleanthes had a sore on his gum, and his doctors told him to fast for two days. When the sore healed and they told him to eat again, he refused with these words, and continued to fast until he died. He chose his end as calmly as he had lived.
p: Look back at how far you have come on some long road. Let it give you calm for what remains.
