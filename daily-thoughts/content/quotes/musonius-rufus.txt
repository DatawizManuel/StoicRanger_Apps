# Lectures 5
q: Practice is more important than theory.
q: Theory teaches how to act; practice is what makes us able to act.
c: Musonius asked his students which was stronger, habit or argument. A doctor who has studied medicine but never treated a patient, or a pilot who knows the theory of sailing but has never held a rudder, is of little use. Ethics, he said, is the same. Knowing what is right is only the beginning; doing it repeatedly is what forms character.
p: Pick one principle you already know is right. Practise it three times today.

# Lectures 3
q: Women have received from the gods the same reason as men.
q: Why then should it be fitting for men to seek and consider how to live well, which is what philosophy is, but not for women?
c: In a culture where women were rarely educated beyond the household, Musonius argued that women have the same senses, the same capacity for reason and the same natural inclination to virtue. A woman who studies philosophy, he said, will be a better manager of her home, a better partner and more just and self-controlled.
p: Think of someone whose view you usually overlook. Ask for it today, and take it seriously.

# Lectures 4
q: Daughters should be educated in the same way as sons.
c: Musonius pressed the argument further than most of his contemporaries. If the virtues of men and women are the same, then their training should be too. Courage, justice and self-control are not male or female; they are human. He did allow that some practical tasks might be divided by strength, but not the education of the mind.
p: Look at how you encourage the young people around you. Are you expecting the same courage and judgment from all of them?

# Lectures 6
q: Since a human being is neither soul alone nor body alone, but a combination of the two,
q: whoever is in training must care for both, the soul more, but the body too.
c: Musonius taught that philosophy requires physical training as well as mental. We should get used to cold, heat, thirst, hunger, plain food and a hard bed. This toughening does not make us better in itself, but it makes the soul steadier, and the body ready to carry out what the mind decides.
p: Do something today that is physically uncomfortable but good for you, and notice how it steadies your mind.

# Lectures 7
q: People endure hard work and pain to win a lover or to make money.
q: Is it not absurd that we will not endure the same for the sake of goodness?
c: Musonius points out how much effort people are willing to make for things of little value: athletes who train for a crown, merchants who risk their lives at sea, soldiers who bear wounds for pay. Virtue is worth far more, and the effort it costs is usually far less.
p: Compare the effort you put into your job or hobby with the effort you put into becoming a better person. Shift some of it today.

# Lectures 8
q: A king must study philosophy,
q: for no one needs to know better what is just and good than the one who rules over others.
c: This lecture is addressed to a king from Syria who came to hear Musonius. Musonius tells him that a ruler should be a model of self-control and justice, should judge fairly and should not be ruled by anger or pleasure. Philosophy is not a private hobby for a ruler but a duty owed to all his subjects.
e: AD 69 · Outside Rome | The lecture to the army | During the civil war that brought Vespasian to power, Musonius went with a peace delegation to meet the advancing troops. He walked among the soldiers, lecturing them on the blessings of peace and the dangers of war. Many mocked him, others found him tiresome, and Tacitus says he had to stop before being beaten. He spoke anyway.
p: If you lead anyone today, at home or at work, ask: what would a just and self-controlled leader do here?
s: Tacitus, Histories 3.81.

# Lectures 9
q: Exile does not take away anything that is truly good.
q: The exile is not prevented from being brave, just or self-controlled.
c: Musonius speaks from experience. He was exiled more than once and argued that exile deprives us only of things like home, friends and status, none of which can make us good or bad. Many famous people, he says, have done their best work far from home.
e: AD 65 · Gyara, Aegean Sea | The island prison | After the conspiracy against Nero, Musonius was banished to Gyara, a rocky and waterless island used for political prisoners. Visitors came by sea to hear him teach. One later account says he found a spring on the island, making it easier to live on. He returned to Rome after Nero's death.
p: If you feel cut off from something today, ask what part of your character it has actually taken away.
s: Gyara: Tacitus, Annals 15.71; Philostratus, Life of Apollonius 7.16.

# Lectures 10
q: To accept an injury without savagery, without being implacable towards the one who did it,
q: to be instead a source of good hope for them, is the mark of a civilised and gentle character.
c: Musonius was asked whether a philosopher should take someone to court for an insult. His answer was no. Being slapped or mocked is not a real harm; what we lose is only an imagined dignity. Instead of revenge, he recommends patience, and even being a source of hope for the person who offended.
p: If someone slights you today, let it go without needing to win.

# Lectures 13A
q: In marriage there must be complete companionship and mutual care between husband and wife,
q: in health and sickness and at all times.
c: Musonius described marriage as a partnership in which both partners share everything, body, possessions and life, and each tries to outdo the other in care. A marriage where each looks only to their own interest falls apart, however beautiful the house. It was a high view of marriage for his time, and an equal one.
p: Do one thing today for your partner, or a close friend, that puts their needs first.

# Lectures 15
q: To have many brothers and sisters is a far greater good
q: than to have many possessions.
c: Musonius opposed the common practice of abandoning unwanted newborns, especially among the wealthy who wanted fewer heirs. Children, brothers and sisters are a greater good than wealth, he argued. A family is a resource no fortune can buy.
p: Spend time today with family or friends, not as a duty, but as the wealth it is.

# Lectures 16
q: If your father forbids you to study philosophy, you need not obey him,
q: for you would be disobeying him only to obey a better father: reason, which is common to all.
c: Musonius asks whether children must always obey their parents. In most things, yes. But obedience means following good advice; if a parent commands something wrong, refusing is the deeper form of respect. A son who becomes good, he says, honours his father more than one who simply does what he is told.
p: Is there a rule you follow only because someone told you to? Ask whether reason supports it.

# Lectures 18A
q: We should eat in order to live, not live in order to eat.
c: Musonius taught that food is the first test of self-control, because we face it several times every day. He recommended simple food that needs little preparation, such as grains, vegetables, fruit, milk and cheese, and warned against gluttony, which makes people resemble pigs and dogs more than humans.
p: At one meal today, eat simply and slowly, and stop when you have had enough.

# Lectures 19
q: We should use clothing and shelter to protect the body, not to impress others.
c: Musonius argued that houses and clothes should serve the needs of the body. Expensive marble, gilded ceilings and fine robes do not make anyone happier, and keeping them up costs time and freedom. A simple house is easier to keep, and leaves money for helping others.
p: Before buying or wearing something today, ask: is this for my needs, or for others' eyes?

# Lectures 11
q: Is not working the land a better and more fitting way of life for a philosopher
q: than living in the city and depending on others?
c: Musonius praised farming as a livelihood for a philosopher. It keeps the body strong, provides an honest living and allows teaching in the open, as students work alongside the teacher and see virtue in action. It is a vision of philosophy lived in daily work rather than in lecture halls alone.
p: Do something with your hands today, and let it be part of your thinking time.

# Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 16.1
q: If you do something good with effort, the effort passes, but the good remains.
q: If you do something shameful with pleasure, the pleasure passes, but the shame remains.
c: Aulus Gellius heard this saying as a young man in Athens and attributes it to Musonius. It shows in two sentences how differently effort and pleasure behave over time. One leaves something lasting behind; the other leaves only what you would rather forget.
p: When faced with a choice today, ask what will remain once the effort or pleasure has passed.
s: Saying attributed to Musonius by Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 16.1.

# Epictetus, Discourses 3.23.29
q: If you have time to praise me, I am speaking to no purpose.
c: Epictetus remembered his teacher's lectures as uncomfortable. Musonius spoke so directly, Epictetus says, that each listener thought someone had told him about their faults. A philosopher's lecture room, Epictetus added, is a doctor's surgery: you should leave feeling pain, not pleasure. Applause meant the medicine had not worked.
p: Ask someone you trust to tell you one thing you could do better, and resist the urge to defend yourself.
s: Saying reported by Epictetus, Discourses 3.23.29.
