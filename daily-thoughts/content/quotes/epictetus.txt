# Handbook 1
q: Some things are up to us, and some are not.
q: Up to us are our judgments, our impulses, our desires and aversions: in a word, whatever is our own doing.
c: These are the first words of the Handbook, the short summary of Epictetus' teaching made by his student Arrian. Everything else follows from this division. Our body, property, reputation and position are not fully up to us; they can be taken away. Our own judgments and choices are. Peace comes from caring most about the second group.
e: c. AD 93 · Rome | Domitian expels the philosophers | The emperor Domitian, suspicious of philosophers who praised the old Stoic martyrs, banished them all from Rome and Italy. Epictetus, by then a free man and a respected teacher, moved to Nicopolis on the coast of north-west Greece and opened a school there. Exile, which he could not control, became the place where his greatest work was done.
p: Make two lists for today's worries: up to me, not up to me. Spend your energy only on the first.

# Handbook 5
q: People are disturbed not by things,
q: but by their judgments about things.
c: Epictetus gives the most extreme example: death. Death is not terrible, he says, or it would have seemed so to Socrates. What is terrible is the judgment that death is terrible. So when we are troubled, we should not blame others but our own judgments. An uneducated person blames others; someone beginning to learn blames themselves; an educated person blames no one.
p: When you're upset today, finish this sentence: "I am upset because I judge that..." Then look at the judgment.

# Handbook 8
q: Do not ask for things to happen as you wish.
q: Wish for them to happen as they do happen, and your life will go well.
c: This is one of the hardest lines in Stoic teaching. Epictetus does not say we should not try to change things. He says we should not make our peace depend on events going our way. Do your part, then accept the outcome as the place where your next action begins.
e: Before AD 68 · Rome | The broken leg | A story told by the early Christian writer Origen says that Epictetus' master Epaphroditus once twisted his leg as a test. Epictetus calmly warned him that it would break. When it did, he said only: "Didn't I tell you it would break?" Whether true or legend, the story became a symbol of how he lived what he taught.
p: Pick one thing today that did not go your way, and practise wanting it exactly as it is, as the starting point for what you do next.
s: Story of the leg: Origen, Against Celsus 7.53. Another ancient source says he was lame from illness.

# Handbook 3
q: With everything that delights you, or is useful, or that you love, remind yourself what kind of thing it is, starting with the smallest.
q: If you are fond of a jug, say: "I am fond of a jug." Then if it breaks, you will not be upset.
c: Epictetus goes on to apply this to the people we love most, reminding ourselves that they are mortal. It sounds cold, but it is the opposite. Knowing that what we love is fragile keeps us from taking it for granted, and keeps loss from shattering us when it comes.
p: With something or someone you love today, pause for a moment and remember that it is not permanent. Then enjoy it more fully.

# Handbook 11
q: Never say of anything, "I have lost it", but rather, "I have given it back".
c: Has your child died? Given back. Your wife? Given back. Your property was taken? That too was given back. Epictetus reminds his students that everything we have is on loan. While it is ours, we should care for it like travellers caring for an inn: not as our own, but as something entrusted to us for a while.
p: Think of something you lost recently. Instead of "I lost it", say "I gave it back", and see how that changes the feeling.

# Handbook 17
q: Remember that you are an actor in a play, whose character the author has chosen.
q: Your task is to play well the part you are given. Choosing the part belongs to another.
c: You may be given the part of a beggar, a king, someone disabled or an ordinary citizen. Epictetus, a former slave who was lame, knew this well. The part is not up to us. How well we play it is.
p: Name the role you are playing today: parent, colleague, friend, leader. Play it as well as you can.

# Handbook 20
q: Remember that it is not the one who insults or strikes you who wrongs you,
q: but your judgment that they are wronging you.
c: When someone provokes you, Epictetus says, know that it is your own judgment that has provoked you. So the first task is not to be carried away by the impression. Gain some time and delay, and you will more easily master yourself.
p: When someone annoys you today, pause and say: "This is an impression, not a fact." Then decide how to respond.

# Handbook 33
q: Be silent for the most part, or say only what is necessary, and in few words.
c: In this chapter Epictetus gives a set of rules for daily conduct. Avoid long talk about gladiators, horse races, athletes, food and drink, and above all about other people, whether praising, blaming or comparing them. Speak when there is a reason; let your words be fewer than your actions.
p: In one conversation today, speak less than usual. Notice what you learn by listening.

# Handbook 46
q: Never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk much among ordinary people about your principles.
q: Act on them instead.
c: Sheep do not bring their grass to the shepherd to show how much they have eaten, Epictetus says. They digest it and produce wool and milk. In the same way, do not show off your principles; show the actions that come from digesting them.
p: Do one thing today that shows your values, without mentioning the values.

# Handbook 48
q: Signs of someone making progress: they blame no one, praise no one, find fault with no one, accuse no one.
q: They say nothing about themselves as if they were someone or knew something.
c: Epictetus describes the student who is truly improving. They are quiet about themselves, slow to blame, unbothered by praise or criticism. If they are praised, they laugh to themselves; if they are blamed, they do not defend themselves. They watch over themselves, Epictetus says, like an enemy lying in wait.
p: For one day, try not to blame anyone, including yourself. Just observe and adjust.

# Handbook 51
q: How long will you wait before you think yourself worthy of the best,
q: and never go against the decision of reason?
c: Epictetus urges his students not to keep postponing their own improvement. You have learned the principles, he says; you are no longer a child. If you keep putting it off, you will live and die as an ordinary person. Socrates became Socrates by paying attention to nothing but reason in everything he did.
e: c. AD 108 · Nicopolis | Arrian takes notes | A young man from Nicomedia, Lucius Flavius Arrianus, studied with Epictetus and wrote down his lectures as nearly word for word as he could, "to preserve the thoughts and frankness of his speech". Arrian later became a Roman consul, a governor and the historian of Alexander the Great. Without his notebook, Epictetus' teaching would be lost.
p: Is there something you keep saying you will start "someday"? Start today.

# Handbook 13
q: If you want to improve, be content to be thought foolish and simple in external matters.
c: Progress often looks odd to others. If you stop chasing what others value, they may think you are naive. Epictetus says to accept that. You cannot keep your inner choice in line with nature and also secure everything outside you; caring for one means neglecting the other.
p: Do one thing today that is right but might look foolish to others.

# Discourses 1.1
q: I must die. Must I die groaning as well?
q: I must be put in chains. Must I complain as well? I must go into exile. Does anyone stop me from going with a smile, cheerful and calm?
c: Epictetus is describing what is up to us and what is not. Death, chains and exile can come from outside. How we meet them is our own. He tells the story of Paconius Agrippinus to show it.
e: AD 66 · Rome | "Let's have lunch at Aricia" | The senator Paconius Agrippinus was on trial before the Senate under Nero. When a messenger came to say his case was being heard, Agrippinus went on with his exercise and bath. When told he had been condemned to exile, he asked only: "Where? To Aricia?" and said, "Then let's go and have lunch there." Aricia was a town a short ride from Rome.
p: When something unwelcome comes today, ask: this I cannot change, but must I add complaint to it?
s: Agrippinus: Epictetus, Discourses 1.1.28–30; Tacitus, Annals 16.28–33.

# Discourses 1.2
q: "Then I will kill you." "When did I ever tell you I was immortal?
q: You will do your part, and I will do mine. Yours is to kill; mine is to die without trembling."
c: Epictetus tells this as the exchange between the emperor Vespasian and the senator Helvidius Priscus, a Stoic who refused to stay away from the Senate or keep quiet there. He uses it to show what it means to keep to one's own role, whatever the cost.
e: c. AD 75 · Rome | Helvidius Priscus | Helvidius Priscus, son-in-law of the Stoic martyr Thrasea Paetus, openly criticised Vespasian and refused to address him by his imperial titles. He was exiled and then executed. For later Stoics he was a model of frank speech, praised by Epictetus and remembered with honour by Marcus Aurelius.
p: Is there something true that you are avoiding saying because it is uncomfortable? Find the right way to say it.
s: Helvidius: Suetonius, Vespasian 15; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 1.14.

# Discourses 1.15
q: Nothing great comes into being all at once; not even a bunch of grapes or a fig.
q: If you tell me you want a fig now, I will answer: it needs time. Let it first blossom, then bear fruit, then ripen.
c: A student asked Epictetus how to stop being angry with his brother. Epictetus answers that a change of character is like ripening fruit. It cannot be done in an hour. Patience with yourself is part of the work.
p: Pick one habit you are trying to change. Instead of judging today's progress, ask only whether the fruit is a little riper.

# Discourses 2.18
q: Every habit and ability is strengthened by its corresponding actions: walking by walking, running by running.
q: If you want to do something, make a habit of it. If you do not want to, do not do it, and get used to doing something else instead.
c: Epictetus explains that anger, desire and every vice grow stronger each time we give in to them, just as muscles grow with use. The good news is that the reverse is also true. Count the days you have not been angry, he advises: every other day, then every third, then every fourth.
p: Choose one bad habit and count today as day one without it. Choose one good habit and do it once.

# Discourses 3.23
q: First tell yourself what you want to be,
q: then do what you have to do.
c: This is the same in every craft, Epictetus says. The athlete first decides what kind of athlete to be, then trains accordingly. The same applies to life. Unless we decide what we want to become, our actions will be random, and we will act without direction.
p: Write one sentence: "I want to be someone who..." Then do one thing today that follows from it.

# Discourses 4.1
q: Freedom is not won by satisfying your desires,
q: but by removing them.
c: Discourses 4.1 is the longest lecture on freedom. Epictetus, a former slave, insists that freedom is something deeper than legal status. Many free people are slaves to wealth, approval and fear. Whoever wants nothing that others control cannot be controlled.
p: Notice one desire today that makes you depend on someone else's approval. Loosen your grip on it.

# Discourses 1.18
q: I lost my lamp because the thief was better at staying awake than I was.
q: But he paid for that lamp: for a lamp, he became a thief; for a lamp, he lost his good faith.
c: Epictetus uses a real loss from his own house to show how to think about being wronged. The thief gained a lamp but lost something far more valuable: his honesty. Epictetus lost only an object. Who has really suffered the loss?
e: After AD 135 · Rome | The lamp of Epictetus | According to the satirist Lucian, after Epictetus' death someone bought his simple clay lamp for three thousand drachmas, hoping that by reading at night by its light he would gain Epictetus' wisdom. Lucian mocks the buyer: wisdom cannot be bought along with a lamp.
p: If something is taken from you today, ask what the other person paid for it.
s: Lamp: Lucian, The Ignorant Book-Collector 13.

# Discourses 1.24
q: It is difficulties that show what people are.
q: When a difficulty comes, remember that God, like a trainer, has matched you with a tough young opponent.
c: Why? asks Epictetus. So that you can become an Olympic victor; and that cannot happen without sweat. Hardship is not a sign that something has gone wrong, but a training partner.
e: 9 September 1965 · North Vietnam | Stockdale ejects | US Navy pilot James Stockdale was shot down over North Vietnam. As he floated down by parachute, he later said, he told himself he was "leaving the world of technology and entering the world of Epictetus". He spent seven and a half years as a prisoner of war, much of it in solitary confinement and under torture, and credited Epictetus' teaching with helping him survive.
p: Treat today's hardest moment as a training partner. What is it training you in?
s: Stockdale: J. B. Stockdale, Courage under Fire (1993).

# Discourses 2.17
q: It is impossible for someone to begin to learn what they think they already know.
c: Epictetus begins this lecture by observing that everyone thinks they know what is good and bad, just and unjust. Because we think we already know, we never examine it. Real learning starts with admitting how much we have assumed.
p: Choose one opinion you hold strongly. Spend five minutes honestly asking whether you might be wrong.

# Discourses 1.9
q: If what philosophers say about the kinship of God and humans is true,
q: what is left for us but to do as Socrates did, and when asked where we are from, never to say "Athens" or "Corinth", but "I am a citizen of the world".
c: Epictetus teaches that all rational beings share in reason and are therefore a single community. National and city identities are real but secondary. A person who understands this will not be humiliated by exile or lowly status, because no place on earth is foreign to them.
p: Treat someone today as you would a fellow citizen, even if you seem to have nothing in common.

# Handbook 43
q: Everything has two handles, one by which it can be carried and one by which it cannot.
q: If your brother wrongs you, do not take it by the handle "he wrongs me". Take it by the other: "he is my brother, raised with me".
c: The same situation can be picked up in different ways. One handle leads to anger and a broken relationship; the other leads to patience and repair. The facts stay the same; the grip changes.
p: In one conflict today, look for the other handle. What else is true about this person or situation?

# Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 17.19
q: Endure and refrain.
c: The Roman writer Aulus Gellius reports that Epictetus summed up his teaching in two words in Greek: anechou kai apechou, "bear and forbear". Endure what must be endured; hold back from what you should not do. Two simple words, almost impossible to fully live by.
p: Today, endure one discomfort without complaint, and refrain from one thing you know you should not do.
s: Saying reported by Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 17.19.

# Discourses 4.12
q: When you let your attention slip for a moment, do not imagine you can get it back whenever you like.
q: Remember this: because of today's slip, everything that follows will be worse.
c: Epictetus warns against the thought "I'll focus tomorrow". Carelessness becomes a habit, he says, and each lapse makes the next one easier. Is there any part of life where inattention does not make things worse? The carpenter, the helmsman and the student all do worse work when they drift.
p: Pick one task today and give it your full attention from start to finish.
