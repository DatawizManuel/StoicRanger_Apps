# The Art of War 1.1–2
q: The art of war is of vital importance to the State.
q: It is a matter of life and death, a road either to safety or to ruin.
c: These are the opening words of the book. Sun Tzu does not glorify war; he treats it as a grave matter that must be studied carefully precisely because so much is at stake. Careless leaders lose nations.
e: 1972 · Yinqueshan, Shandong | The bamboo slips | Workers digging near Linyi opened two Han dynasty tombs from around 140 BC. Inside were nearly five thousand bamboo strips covered with writing, including large parts of The Art of War and a long-lost work by the later strategist Sun Bin. The find proved that the text we read today was already established more than two thousand years ago.
p: Name the one decision in your life right now that is truly a "road to safety or to ruin". Give it the serious study it deserves.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 3.2
q: To fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence;
q: supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting.
c: This is Sun Tzu's most famous principle. Winning a battle costs lives, money and time, even for the winner. The best victory is one where the other side yields without a fight, through strategy, alliances or a position too strong to challenge.
p: In one disagreement today, look for a way to resolve it without a fight.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 3.18
q: If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.
q: If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat.
c: Sun Tzu continues: if you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will lose every battle. Knowledge, not force, decides outcomes. And notice the order: knowing yourself is as important as knowing the other side.
e: 1910 · London | Giles translates Sun Tzu | Lionel Giles, a curator of Chinese books at the British Museum, published his translation of The Art of War with extensive notes, criticising an earlier English version for its mistakes. It became the standard English text for decades and is now in the public domain. The words on this page are his.
p: Before a meeting or challenge today, write down three honest facts about yourself and three about the other side.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 4.1–2
q: The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy.
q: To secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself.
c: This passage is strikingly Stoic. Sun Tzu separates what is up to us, our own preparation and defence, from what is not, the enemy's mistakes. Make yourself solid first; then be ready when an opening appears.
p: Strengthen one thing today that is fully in your hands, and stay alert for openings you cannot force.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 1.26
q: Now the general who wins a battle makes many calculations in his temple ere the battle is fought.
q: The general who loses a battle makes but few calculations beforehand.
c: Before a campaign, generals in ancient China held councils in the ancestral temple to compare their strengths with the enemy's. Sun Tzu says that victory is decided there, by careful thinking, long before the armies meet.
e: 1772 · Paris | The Jesuit's translation | Jean Joseph Marie Amiot, a French Jesuit missionary living at the Qing court in Beijing, sent home a French translation of The Art of War. It was the first version in a European language and was read in France in the years before the Revolution. Some later writers claimed Napoleon had studied it, though there is no proof.
p: Before your next important task, spend ten minutes planning it in detail.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 2.6
q: There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare.
c: Sun Tzu warns that long campaigns drain the treasury, exhaust the people and invite other enemies to take advantage. Speed and decisiveness are kinder in the end than drawn-out conflict. The same holds true of disputes in daily life.
p: Is there a conflict in your life that has dragged on too long? Take a step to end it.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 4.13
q: He wins his battles by making no mistakes.
c: Sun Tzu explains that the truly skilled general does not win spectacular victories that everyone praises. His wins look easy, because he has prepared so well that the outcome was never in doubt. Excellence often looks boring from the outside.
p: Choose one task today and do it so carefully that nothing goes wrong.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 4.15
q: In war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won,
q: whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory.
c: This line reverses the usual idea of battle. The winner wins in preparation and only then fights. The loser fights first and hopes to find a way to win along the way.
p: Before you start something important today, ask whether you have already won it in preparation.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 5.13–15
q: The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim.
q: Energy may be likened to the bending of a crossbow; decision, to the releasing of a trigger.
c: Sun Tzu distinguishes stored energy from the moment of action. A crossbow drawn too early wastes strength; released too late, the chance is gone. The skill is in building up force patiently and then acting at exactly the right moment.
p: Identify one decision you have been holding back. If the moment is right, release the trigger today.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 6.31–32
q: Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows.
q: Just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions.
c: Water flows around obstacles and finds the weakest point. Sun Tzu urges the same flexibility: do not hold rigidly to one plan; read the situation and adapt. The commander who can change his tactics according to the enemy "may be called a heaven-born captain".
p: When a plan meets resistance today, flow around it instead of pushing harder.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 6.1–2
q: Whoever is first in the field and awaits the coming of the enemy, will be fresh for the fight.
q: The clever combatant imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him.
c: Being early is an advantage in war and in life. The one who arrives first chooses the ground, rests and prepares. The one who arrives late must react to someone else's plan. Taking the initiative means setting the terms.
p: Arrive early today at one appointment or task, and use the extra time to prepare.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 7.17–19
q: Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest.
q: In raiding and plundering be like fire, in immovability like a mountain. Let your plans be dark and impenetrable as night, and when you move, fall like a thunderbolt.
c: Sun Tzu uses images from nature to describe the qualities of a good army: fast when it must move, solid when it must hold, fierce in attack, steady in defence. A good person, like a good army, needs different qualities for different moments.
e: 16th century · Kai Province, Japan | Fūrinkazan | The Japanese warlord Takeda Shingen took four words from this passage, Wind, Forest, Fire, Mountain, and carried them on his battle standard. His banner with the motto "Fūrinkazan" became one of the most famous in Japanese history, a sign of how far Sun Tzu's ideas travelled.
p: Ask which quality today needs from you: wind, forest, fire or mountain.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 7.31
q: To be near the goal while the enemy is still far from it, to wait at ease while the enemy is toiling and struggling,
q: to be well-fed while the enemy is famished: this is the art of husbanding one's strength.
c: Sun Tzu stresses the importance of conserving energy. Exhaustion defeats armies as surely as weapons. Rest, food and position are not luxuries but weapons in themselves.
p: Protect your energy today. Rest, eat well and do not waste effort on what does not matter.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 8.11
q: The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him;
q: not on the chance of his not attacking, but rather on the fact that we have made our position unassailable.
c: Hope is not a strategy. Sun Tzu says we should not count on trouble staying away, but on being ready when it comes. This is the military version of the Stoic practice of preparing in advance for hardship.
p: Prepare today for one problem you are hoping will not happen.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 8.12
q: There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: recklessness, which leads to destruction; cowardice, which leads to capture;
q: a hasty temper, which can be provoked by insults; a delicacy of honor which is sensitive to shame; over-solicitude for his men, which exposes him to worry and trouble.
c: Sun Tzu lists five weaknesses that an enemy can exploit. Each is a virtue pushed too far or a passion left unchecked. A Stoic would recognise them all as errors of judgment that a trained mind can correct.
p: Which of the five faults is closest to you? Watch for it today.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 9.43
q: Soldiers must be treated in the first instance with humanity,
q: but kept under control by means of iron discipline.
c: Sun Tzu combines care and firmness. Soldiers punished before they are attached to their leader will be resentful; soldiers who are attached but never disciplined will be useless. Good leadership needs both.
e: c. 512 BC · Palace of Wu | The drill of the palace women | According to the historian Sima Qian, King Helü of Wu tested Sun Tzu by asking him to drill 180 palace women. When they laughed at his orders, Sun Tzu explained again; when they laughed again, he had the king's two favourite concubines, who led the companies, executed. After that, the women obeyed perfectly. The king, though grieving, appointed him general.
p: Be kind and firm with someone today: warm in manner, clear in expectations.
s: Palace drill: Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian 65.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 10.24
q: The general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace,
q: whose only thought is to protect his country and do good service for his sovereign, is the jewel of the kingdom.
c: Sun Tzu's ideal commander is free from vanity. He advances when it is right, not to win glory, and retreats when it is right, even if people call him a coward. His judgment is not bent by reputation.
p: Make one decision today based on what is right, not on how it will look.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 10.25
q: Regard your soldiers as your children, and they will follow you into the deepest valleys;
q: look upon them as your own beloved sons, and they will stand by you even unto death.
c: Sun Tzu adds a warning: if you are indulgent but cannot make your authority felt, they will be like spoilt children, useless for any practical purpose. Care and authority go together.
p: Treat someone you lead or support today with the care you would give your own family.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 12.18–19
q: No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique.
q: If it is to your advantage, make a forward move; if not, stay where you are.
c: Anger is a bad reason for any action. Sun Tzu warns rulers not to start wars, and generals not to fight battles, because they feel insulted. Act when it helps; otherwise, wait.
p: Before acting on irritation today, ask whether it actually serves your goal.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 12.20–22
q: Anger may in time change to gladness; vexation may be succeeded by content.
q: But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life.
c: Feelings pass, but some consequences are permanent. This is why the enlightened ruler is careful and the good general cautious. Do not let a passing mood make an irreversible decision.
p: If you feel tempted to do something irreversible in anger today, wait until the feeling passes.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 13.4–6
q: What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge.
q: Now this foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.
c: In the last chapter, on spies, Sun Tzu insists that knowledge must come from real information, not from omens, guesswork or theory. Spending money on good information is far cheaper than losing a war through ignorance.
p: Before making a decision today, find out one fact from someone who actually knows.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 3.17
q: He will win who knows when to fight and when not to fight.
c: This is the first of Sun Tzu's five essentials for victory. Not every battle should be fought. Recognising when to engage and when to step back is itself a victory, and often the most important one.
p: Choose one battle not to fight today.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 5.10–11
q: In battle, there are not more than two methods of attack, the direct and the indirect; yet these two in combination give rise to an endless series of maneuvers.
q: The direct and the indirect lead on to each other in turn. It is like moving in a circle: you never come to an end.
c: Sun Tzu compares strategy to music, colour and taste: just five notes, five colours and five flavours, yet their combinations are endless. A few basic principles, combined creatively, produce limitless possibilities.
p: Solve one problem today with an indirect approach instead of a head-on one.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 11.29–30
q: The skillful tactician may be likened to the shuai-jan. Now the shuai-jan is a snake that is found in the Ch'ang mountains.
q: Strike at its head, and you will be attacked by its tail; strike at its tail, and you will be attacked by its head; strike at its middle, and you will be attacked by head and tail both.
c: Sun Tzu uses this image for a well-coordinated army in which every part supports the others. He adds that even enemies, like the men of Wu and Yue, will help each other when caught in the same boat in a storm. Unity is strength.
p: Support a teammate or family member today without being asked.
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).

# The Art of War 1.18–19
q: All warfare is based on deception.
q: When able to attack, we must seem unable; when using our forces, we must seem inactive.
c: Sun Tzu's most notorious line is a warning as much as advice. In conflict, things are often not what they seem. The Stoic lesson is to look beyond appearances, both in others and in our own first impressions.
p: Look twice today at something that seems obvious. What might the appearance be hiding?
t: Translation: Lionel Giles, 1910 (public domain).
