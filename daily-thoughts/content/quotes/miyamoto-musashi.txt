# Dokkōdō, precept 1
q: Accept everything just the way it is.
c: The Dokkōdō, "The Way of Walking Alone", is a list of twenty-one short precepts that Musashi wrote a week before his death. Its first line could have been written by Epictetus. Acceptance is not giving up; it is the starting point for clear action, because we cannot act well on a situation we refuse to see.
e: May 1645 · Kumamoto | The last week | Knowing he was dying, Musashi gave away his possessions, wrote the Dokkōdō and handed it, together with The Book of Five Rings, to his student Terao Magonojō. He died on 13 June 1645, aged about sixty-one. Legend says he was buried in full armour.
p: Choose one thing today that you have been resisting, and accept it exactly as it is. Then decide what to do.

# Dokkōdō, precept 4
q: Think lightly of yourself
q: and deeply of the world.
c: Musashi warns against self-importance. A person preoccupied with themselves misses what is going on around them. Taking yourself lightly frees your attention for what matters.
p: In one moment today when you feel offended or important, turn your attention outward instead.

# Dokkōdō, precept 6
q: Do not regret what you have done.
c: For Musashi, regret wastes the energy needed for the next action. This does not mean ignoring mistakes; he studied his defeats and errors carefully. It means not carrying them as weight. Learn the lesson, then put the burden down.
p: Take one past mistake you keep replaying. Name the lesson, and let the rest go.

# Dokkōdō, precept 7
q: Never be jealous.
c: Jealousy fixes our attention on what others have instead of on our own path. Musashi, who walked his own way all his life, saw it as a distraction from the work of self-improvement.
p: When you notice envy today, turn it into a question: what is one thing I can do on my own path?

# Dokkōdō, precept 9
q: Resentment and complaint are fitting
q: neither for yourself nor for others.
c: Musashi lived a hard life: no lord for most of it, little money, constant danger. He gives himself no room to complain. Complaint changes nothing and poisons the mind.
p: Go one full day without complaining, aloud or in your head.

# Dokkōdō, precept 14
q: Do not keep possessions
q: you no longer need.
c: Musashi lived simply and travelled light. Possessions, he believed, tie us down and occupy the mind. In his last days he gave away everything he owned.
p: Give away or throw out one thing today that you no longer need.

# Dokkōdō, precept 17
q: Do not fear death.
c: Musashi faced death in dozens of duels from the age of thirteen. He came to see the fear of death as something that weakens judgment in the moment it is needed most. The Stoics and the samurai agreed: a mind free from the fear of death is free.
e: 1596 · Harima Province | The first duel | According to the opening of The Book of Five Rings, Musashi fought his first duel at thirteen, against Arima Kihei, a samurai of the Shintō-ryū school who had posted a public challenge. The boy accepted, and killed him. It was the first of more than sixty duels he claimed never to have lost.
p: When fear rises today, ask what you would do if you were not afraid. Then do it.

# Dokkōdō, precept 19
q: Respect the Buddha and the gods
q: without counting on their help.
c: Musashi honoured the gods but relied on his own effort. Another story tells that before a duel he stopped at a shrine to pray for victory, then turned away, realising that he should depend on himself alone. Reverence and self-reliance can go together.
p: Do your part today as if everything depends on you.

# Dokkōdō, precept 21
q: Never stray from the Way.
c: This is the last of Musashi's twenty-one precepts. The Way, for him, was the path of strategy and self-discipline he had followed all his life. It meant constant training, clear perception and self-reliance, in war and in every art.
p: Name your own Way in one sentence. Take one step on it today.

# Dokkōdō, precept 12
q: Be indifferent
q: to where you live.
c: Musashi spent most of his life wandering, without a fixed home or a permanent lord. He learned to be comfortable anywhere. In his last years he lived in a mountain cave. Like the Stoic citizen of the world, he did not tie his peace of mind to a place.
e: 1643 · Reigandō, near Kumamoto | The cave | In his sixtieth year, Musashi withdrew to Reigandō, the "Spirit Rock Cave", in the mountains west of Kumamoto, to write The Book of Five Rings. He began writing on the tenth day of the tenth month, after praying to Heaven and to Kannon. The cave can still be visited today.
p: Find calm today wherever you are, without needing a better place.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: Do not think dishonestly.
q: The Way is in training.
c: These are the first two of Musashi's nine principles for those who want to learn his strategy. Honesty with oneself comes first, because self-deception ruins judgment. Then training: the Way is not learned from books but through constant practice.
p: Be completely honest with yourself about one thing today, then train it.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: Perceive those things which cannot be seen.
c: Another of Musashi's nine principles. The skilled strategist notices what others miss: intentions, rhythms, the moment before something happens. This comes only through long practice and careful attention.
p: In one conversation today, pay attention to what is not said.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: Pay attention even to trifles.
q: Do nothing which is of no use.
c: These are the last two of the nine principles. Small details decide outcomes, and wasted effort weakens us. Musashi's way is economical: full attention, no wasted movement.
p: Today, cut one useless activity and give that attention to a small detail that matters.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: From one thing, know ten thousand things.
c: Musashi believed that mastering one discipline deeply teaches principles that apply everywhere. The way of the sword taught him painting, sculpture, calligraphy and leadership. Deep understanding of one thing opens many doors.
p: Take a principle from your work or hobby and apply it to another part of your life today.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: The master carpenter uses his men according to their abilities.
q: He knows who is fit for the pillars, who for the floors, who for the rough work.
c: Musashi compares the strategist to a master builder who assigns each worker and each piece of wood to the place where it serves best. A good leader knows people's strengths and puts them where they can succeed.
p: Recognise one person's real strength today and give them a chance to use it.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: When I passed thirty, I looked back and saw that my victories had not come from mastery of strategy.
q: I went on studying morning and evening, and only at about fifty did I come to understand the Way.
c: Musashi admits that his early victories came from natural talent, luck or the weakness of his opponents. Undefeated, he kept training for another twenty years until he understood what he was doing. Success did not end his search.
e: 1612 · Funashima (Ganryū-jima) | The duel with Kojirō | Musashi's most famous duel was fought against Sasaki Kojirō, known for his very long sword, on a small island in the strait between Honshu and Kyushu. As the story is usually told, Musashi arrived late, having carved a wooden sword from a spare oar on the boat, and killed Kojirō with a single blow. Many details are later legend.
p: Look at one thing you are good at and ask how much of it is real understanding. Keep learning.

# The Book of Five Rings, Earth
q: You should not have a favourite weapon.
q: Too much of one thing is as bad as not enough.
c: Musashi warns against over-reliance on any single tool or method. A strategist who has a favourite becomes predictable and is helpless when it fails. Flexibility is strength.
p: Try a different approach to a familiar task today.

# The Book of Five Rings, Water
q: Water adopts the shape of its vessel;
q: it is sometimes a trickle and sometimes a wild sea.
c: Musashi named the second book after water because the mind should be like it: flexible, clear and adaptable. A rigid mind breaks; a mind like water adjusts to every situation. Sun Tzu used the same image.
p: Let your plan bend today to fit the situation, instead of forcing the situation into your plan.

# The Book of Five Rings, Water
q: In strategy, your state of mind must not be any different from normal.
q: Keep your mind broad and straight, neither too tense nor too relaxed.
c: Musashi says the mind should be the same in combat as in daily life: calm, open, balanced. Excitement and fear distort judgment. The trained mind does not change under pressure.
p: In a stressful moment today, notice your state of mind and bring it back to normal.

# The Book of Five Rings, Water
q: Perception is strong, sight is weak.
q: See distant things as if they were close, and close things as if they were distant.
c: Musashi distinguishes two kinds of seeing: ken, ordinary looking at details, and kan, deeper perception of the whole situation. A good fighter does not fix their eyes on the opponent's sword, but takes in everything at once.
p: Step back from one problem today and look at it as if from far away.

# The Book of Five Rings, Water
q: A thousand days of training to forge,
q: ten thousand days of training to polish.
c: Musashi uses the image of a sword blade, forged and then polished, for the training of a fighter. A thousand days is about three years; ten thousand days, about thirty. Real mastery takes a lifetime.
p: Do one session of patient practice today, knowing that it is one of ten thousand.

# The Book of Five Rings, Fire
q: To become the enemy:
q: think yourself into the enemy's position.
c: Musashi teaches the strategist to see the situation through the opponent's eyes. What do they fear? What do they expect? Understanding the other side changes everything. Outside of combat, this is simply empathy.
p: In one disagreement today, describe the situation from the other person's side.

# The Book of Five Rings, Fire
q: If you have tried something twice and it has failed, do not try it a third time.
q: If the enemy expects the mountain, attack like the sea; if they expect the sea, attack like the mountain.
c: Musashi calls this "mountain and sea". Repeating what has failed is stubbornness, not persistence. A wise strategist changes approach completely when the first one does not work.
p: Is there something you keep trying in the same way? Try something completely different today.

# The Book of Five Rings, Fire
q: Rat's head, ox's neck.
c: Musashi uses this odd phrase to describe two modes of attention. The rat's head is small, fine detail; the ox's neck is the large, strong picture. When you get lost in details, switch to the big picture, and back again. A good strategist moves between them.
p: If you feel lost in details today, step back to the big picture, and vice versa.

# The Book of Five Rings, Void
q: In the void is virtue, and no evil.
q: Wisdom has existence, principle has existence, the Way has existence, spirit is nothingness.
c: The last and shortest book of the Five Rings speaks of the void: the state of mind that is clear, empty of confusion and unattached. For Musashi, after a lifetime of training, the void is where true understanding lives.
p: Sit in silence for five minutes today, letting your mind clear.
