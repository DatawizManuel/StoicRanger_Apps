# Letter to Menoeceus 125
q: Death is nothing to us.
q: When we exist, death is not present; and when death is present, we do not exist.
c: Epicurus believed that most human misery comes from fear, and the greatest fear is of death. His answer is simple. All good and bad lie in sensation, and death is the end of sensation. Where death is, we are not. What is painless when it comes should not pain us in advance.
p: When a fear of the end, of anything, appears today, remind yourself that it is the fear, not the end, that is hurting you.

# Philodemus, Against the Sophists 4.9–14
q: God is not to be feared; death is not to be dreaded;
q: what is good is easy to get; what is terrible is easy to endure.
c: This four-part summary, later called the tetrapharmakos or "four-part cure", was used by Epicureans as a quick reminder of the whole philosophy. It was written down by the Epicurean Philodemus, whose library was buried by Vesuvius at Herculaneum and partly recovered from the charred scrolls.
p: Repeat the four-part cure once today when something unsettles you.

# Letter to Menoeceus 132
q: It is impossible to live pleasantly without living wisely, well and justly,
q: and impossible to live wisely, well and justly without living pleasantly.
c: Critics accused Epicurus of teaching selfish pleasure-seeking. This passage shows the opposite. For him, a truly pleasant life and a virtuous life are inseparable. The virtues are the means to a calm and happy life, and a calm and happy life is impossible without them.
p: Notice one moment today where doing the right thing also brought you peace.

# Letter to Menoeceus 131
q: When we say that pleasure is the goal, we do not mean the pleasures of the dissolute or of the table,
q: but freedom from pain in the body and from disturbance in the soul.
c: Epicurus explicitly rejects the popular picture of an "epicure". Drinking parties, feasts and luxury do not make a pleasant life, he says, but sober reasoning, which examines what to choose and avoid and drives out the opinions that disturb the soul.
p: Today, seek one moment of real calm rather than one moment of excitement.

# Letter to Menoeceus 122
q: Let no one delay the study of philosophy while young, nor grow weary of it when old.
q: For no one is too young or too old to secure the health of the soul.
c: Epicurus opens his letter to Menoeceus with this invitation. Philosophy is not a subject for specialists but medicine for everyone. The young need it to face the future without fear, the old to remain young in the good things they have.
p: Whatever your age, spend some time today thinking about how to live well.

# Principal Doctrines 27
q: Of all the things that wisdom provides for the happiness of a whole life,
q: by far the greatest is friendship.
c: Epicurus built his whole community around friendship. In the Garden, people lived, ate and studied together. He believed that a life without friends, even with every comfort, would be poor and anxious, and that friendship was the surest protection against life's dangers.
e: c. 306 BC · Athens | The Garden | Epicurus bought a house with a garden just outside the walls of Athens for eighty minae and settled there with his friends. The Garden admitted women and slaves, which shocked contemporaries, and became the centre of a network of Epicurean communities across the Greek world. In his will he left it to his followers for ever.
p: Reach out today to a friend you haven't spoken to in a while.

# Vatican Sayings 34
q: We do not so much need the help of our friends
q: as the confidence that they will help us.
c: Epicurus saw that much of friendship's value lies in security. Even if we never call on them, knowing that someone would stand by us makes life calmer and braver.
p: Let one friend know today that they can count on you.

# Vatican Sayings 35
q: Do not spoil what you have by desiring what you do not have.
q: Remember that what you now have was once among the things you only hoped for.
c: Desire has a way of erasing gratitude. As soon as we have something, we start wanting the next thing. Epicurus suggests looking back: much of what we take for granted was once a dream.
p: Name three things you have today that you once only hoped for.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 21.7
q: If you want to make Pythocles rich,
q: do not add to his money, but take away from his desires.
c: Seneca quotes this advice that Epicurus gave about his young friend Pythocles. Wealth is a relationship between what you have and what you want. You can increase it by getting more, or more reliably by wanting less.
p: Let go of one desire today, instead of trying to satisfy it.
s: Quoted by Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 21.7.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 4.10
q: Poverty brought into line with the law of nature
q: is great wealth.
c: Seneca loved this line and quoted it to Lucilius. Nature asks for little: food, water, shelter, friends. If we live by that measure, we are rich. If we live by the measure of opinion and fashion, we will never have enough.
p: Spend one part of today using only what you really need.
s: Quoted by Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 4.10; compare Vatican Sayings 25.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 9.20
q: Whoever does not think what they have is more than enough
q: is unhappy, even if they are master of the whole world.
c: Another saying of Epicurus preserved by Seneca. Happiness depends less on the size of what we have than on our attitude toward it. Alexander conquered half the world and still wept that there were no more worlds to conquer.
p: Say once today, about something you own: "This is enough."
s: Quoted by Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 9.20.

# Vatican Sayings 52
q: Friendship dances around the world,
q: calling on each of us to wake up to happiness.
c: One of the most poetic lines of Epicurus. He imagines friendship as a messenger travelling across the earth, inviting everyone to the blessed life. Happiness is not found alone but in shared life.
p: Invite someone to share something good with you today.

# Principal Doctrines 15
q: Natural wealth is limited and easy to obtain.
q: The wealth demanded by empty opinion goes on without end.
c: Epicurus divided desires into natural and necessary ones (food, shelter), natural but unnecessary ones (fine food), and empty ones (fame, power, luxury). Natural needs are easy to meet. Empty desires can never be satisfied, because they have no natural limit.
p: Before you buy or chase something today, ask whether it is a natural need or an empty desire.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 10.11
q: Send me a little pot of cheese,
q: so that I can have a feast whenever I like.
c: Epicurus wrote this to a friend. It shows how simple his idea of pleasure was. His daily diet was mostly bread and water, and a bit of cheese was a feast. A person who enjoys simple things is easy to please and hard to make miserable.
p: Treat yourself today to one very simple pleasure, and enjoy it fully.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 10.22
q: On this blessed day, which is also the last of my life, I write to you.
q: My pains are extreme, but against them all I set the joy in my soul at the memory of our conversations.
c: These are the opening words of Epicurus' last letter, written to his friend Idomeneus. He asks him to look after the children of his friend Metrodorus, as worthy of his lifelong devotion to Epicurus and philosophy. Even dying in pain, he looked for the good and thought of others.
e: 270 BC · Athens | The death of Epicurus | Epicurus died at seventy-two after fourteen days of severe pain from a kidney stone. According to one account, he had a warm bath, drank some unmixed wine, urged his friends to remember his teachings and died. His will provided for the Garden, his slaves' freedom and an annual feast in his memory.
p: If you are in pain or trouble today, call to mind one good memory with a friend and hold it.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 11.8
q: Choose some good person and keep them always before your eyes,
q: so that you live as if they were watching and act as if they could see.
c: Seneca, a Stoic, quotes this Epicurean advice with full approval. Having a model in mind, real or imagined, helps us behave better when no one is watching. Seneca suggests Cato or Laelius; any good person will do.
p: Choose someone you admire. Go through today as if they were watching.
s: Quoted by Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 11.8.

# Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 28.9
q: The knowledge of your wrongdoing
q: is the beginning of your healing.
c: Seneca quotes this line from Epicurus. We cannot correct a fault we do not see. Many people defend their mistakes instead of recognising them. Self-improvement begins when we are honest with ourselves.
p: Admit one small fault to yourself today, without excuses.
s: Quoted by Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 28.9.

# Vatican Sayings 14
q: We are born once and cannot be born twice, and we will not exist for all eternity.
q: Yet you, who are not master of tomorrow, keep putting off your happiness.
c: Epicurus sees how much of life we waste in postponement: we will be happy when we get the job, the house, the holiday. Life slips by in waiting. Joy has to be found now, because now is all we have.
p: Stop waiting for one thing today. Find a reason to be content right now.

# Vatican Sayings 41
q: We must laugh and do philosophy and manage our households and our other affairs,
q: all at the same time.
c: Philosophy, for Epicurus, was not a separate activity done in a quiet room. It belonged in the middle of daily life, together with humour, work and family. A good life is not divided into compartments.
p: Bring some philosophy and some laughter into your daily tasks today.

# Vatican Sayings 54
q: We must not pretend to study philosophy, but really study it.
q: For we need not the appearance of health, but real health.
c: Epicurus saw philosophy as medicine for the soul. Showing off knowledge is like pretending to be healthy: it does nothing. What counts is the real change in how we live.
p: Apply one idea you have read recently to an actual situation today.

# Vatican Sayings 66
q: Let us show our feeling for friends who are suffering,
q: not by lamenting, but by caring for them.
c: When friends suffer, tears are not enough. Epicurus teaches that compassion should be active. Practical help and thoughtful attention mean more than sympathy alone.
p: If someone you know is going through a hard time, do one practical thing for them today.

# Principal Doctrines 17
q: The just person is the most free from disturbance;
q: the unjust person is full of the greatest disturbance.
c: Epicurus argued that justice is good for us, not just for others. The unjust person lives in fear of discovery and punishment, never at peace. The just person can sleep calmly.
p: Clear up one small matter today where you have been less than fair.

# Plutarch, Is "Live Unnoticed" Well Said? 1128c
q: Live unnoticed.
c: In Greek, lathe biōsas. It was one of Epicurus' best-known maxims, so famous that Plutarch wrote a whole essay attacking it. Epicurus advised staying out of politics and the pursuit of fame. A quiet, private life with friends is safer and more peaceful than a life in the public eye.
p: Do one good thing today without seeking any recognition for it.
s: Maxim quoted and criticised by Plutarch in the essay of that title.

# Letter to Menoeceus 130
q: We regard self-sufficiency as a great good,
q: so that we may be content with little, knowing that those who need luxury least enjoy it most.
c: Epicurus did not reject luxury entirely. But he argued that people who can live simply enjoy rich things more when they come, and are not hurt when they go. Simple living is not deprivation but freedom.
p: Choose the simpler option once today, and notice whether you lose anything real.

# Vatican Sayings 81
q: The disturbance of the soul cannot be ended, nor real joy produced,
q: by the greatest wealth, nor by honour and respect from the crowd.
c: Epicurus saw that external success does not reach the source of anxiety. A troubled mind remains troubled in a palace. Only understanding, and the removal of false opinions, brings real peace.
p: Notice one way you are trying to fix an inner problem with an outer solution today.
