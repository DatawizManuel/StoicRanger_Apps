# Moralia, On Listening to Lectures
q: The mind does not need filling like a jar.
q: Like firewood, it needs only kindling, to set it burning with the desire to think for itself and to love the truth.
c: Plutarch wrote this essay for a young man starting to attend philosophy lectures. Listening well, he says, is half of learning. But the aim of education is not to pour facts into a student; it is to light a fire. This line, usually shortened to "the mind is not a vessel to be filled but a fire to be kindled", has been quoted by teachers ever since.
p: Instead of absorbing more information today, take one idea and think it through for yourself.

# Life of Alexander 1
q: It is not always in the most famous deeds that virtue or vice is revealed.
q: Often a small thing, a word or a joke, shows character more clearly than battles where thousands fall.
c: Plutarch explains his method at the start of the Life of Alexander. He is not writing history but lives. Like a portrait painter who focuses on the face and eyes, he looks for the small signs that reveal the soul.
p: Pay attention today to the small things you do when no one is impressed. They are your real portrait.

# Life of Pericles 1–2
q: Virtue, by its very deeds, so moves us
q: that we admire the actions and at the same time want to imitate those who did them.
c: Plutarch wrote his Lives to give readers examples to follow. Admiring a fine sculpture doesn't make us want to be a sculptor, he says, but admiring a good action makes us want to act well. Good examples are contagious.
p: Think of someone whose actions you admire. Imitate one of them today.

# Life of Aemilius Paulus 1
q: I began writing these Lives for the sake of others,
q: but I continue them for my own sake, using history as a mirror to arrange and adorn my own life.
c: Plutarch admits that his long work on famous lives became a personal exercise. Spending time with great people of the past, he says, is like living with them: we receive each as a guest and study who they were. It teaches us to drive away anything base from our own lives.
p: Read about one life today, historical or personal, and ask what it shows you about your own.

# Life of Theseus 23
q: The ship on which Theseus sailed was kept by the Athenians, who removed old planks as they decayed and put in new ones,
q: so that philosophers took it as an example in the argument about growth: some said it remained the same ship, others that it did not.
c: Plutarch records the famous puzzle known as the Ship of Theseus. If every part is gradually replaced, is it still the same ship? The question applies to us too: our bodies, habits and ideas change constantly. What makes us the same person?
p: Name one part of yourself you have replaced over the years. Are you still the same? What makes you so?

# Moralia, On Tranquility of Mind
q: Instead of always looking at those who are better off,
q: look also at those who are worse off, and you will find much to be grateful for.
c: Plutarch wrote this essay for his friend Paccius, a busy Roman official. He observes that people make themselves miserable by comparing upward. A prisoner envies the free; the free envy the rich; the rich envy kings. Turning the comparison around brings contentment.
p: When you find yourself comparing upward today, make one comparison downward as well.

# Moralia, On Tranquility of Mind
q: The universe is a most holy temple,
q: and every day is a festival for a good person.
c: Plutarch ends his essay on tranquillity with this image, borrowed from a saying of Diogenes the Cynic. Life is like a great festival, and we are guests admitted to see the sun, moon and stars. Why spend it complaining?
p: Treat today as a festival. Find one moment to celebrate.

# Moralia, On Moral Progress
q: A sign of progress is when you are no longer ashamed to be corrected,
q: but rather glad of it, as a sick person is glad of a doctor.
c: Plutarch describes the signs that someone is really improving. One of the clearest is how they react to criticism. Beginners defend themselves; those making progress welcome correction because it helps them heal.
p: Ask someone today for honest feedback, and thank them for it.

# Moralia, On the Control of Anger
q: Just as with a fever, it is easier to stop anger at its start.
q: When it is beginning, keep silent, and do not fan it with words.
c: Plutarch's essay on anger takes the form of a dialogue with a friend who has learned to control his temper. The key advice is to act early: anger feeds on speech. Silence starves it.
p: The next time anger rises today, keep silent for a full minute before responding.

# Moralia, How to Profit by One's Enemies
q: How shall I defend myself against my enemy?
q: By being good and honourable yourself.
c: Plutarch quotes Diogenes the Cynic and builds an essay around the idea. An enemy is a free critic who watches everything you do. Instead of resenting them, use them: let their attention make you more careful, honest and self-controlled.
p: Think of someone who criticises you. Use one of their criticisms to improve today.

# Moralia, On Talkativeness
q: Often we regret having spoken,
q: but never having kept silent.
c: Plutarch's essay on talkativeness is full of stories of secrets betrayed and lives ruined by loose tongues. He quotes the poet Simonides, who said he had often regretted speaking but never silence.
p: Hold back one comment today that you might regret.

# Moralia, Consolation to His Wife
q: Let us not forget the joy she brought us.
q: It would be unjust to remember only the grief and to cancel all the happiness she gave.
c: Plutarch wrote to his wife Timoxena after the death of their two-year-old daughter, also named Timoxena. He praises his wife's calm and asks her to remember their daughter with gratitude, not just pain. It is one of the most moving letters to survive from the ancient world.
e: c. AD 90 · Tanagra and Chaeronea | The death of little Timoxena | Plutarch was travelling when the news reached him that his little daughter had died. Their only daughter after four sons, she had been loved for her gentle nature: she asked her nurse to feed her dolls too. Plutarch's letter tells his wife that he has heard how she bore the loss with dignity and without empty display.
p: When you think of someone you've lost, choose to remember one joyful moment with them today.

# Moralia, On Exile
q: By nature there is no such thing as a native country,
q: any more than there is by nature a house, a field or a forge.
c: Plutarch wrote this to a friend who had been exiled. Borders are drawn by people; the sky, the sun and the stars are the same everywhere. Socrates, he recalls, said that he was not an Athenian or a Greek, but a citizen of the world.
p: Think of yourself today as a citizen of the world. How does that change one decision?

# Moralia, On the E at Delphi
q: Know yourself.
q: Nothing in excess.
c: These two sayings were carved at the temple of Apollo at Delphi, where Plutarch served as priest. He discusses them, and the mysterious letter E also inscribed there, in several dialogues. For Plutarch they summed up the wisdom of Greece: self-knowledge and moderation.
e: c. AD 95–120 · Delphi | Priest of Apollo | For around thirty years Plutarch served as one of the two priests of Apollo at Delphi, the most famous oracle of the ancient world, then in decline. He helped restore the sanctuary, and the people of Delphi and Chaeronea set up a portrait of him in gratitude. The bust on his coin is thought to be it.
p: Spend a moment today on each of the Delphic sayings: one thing you know about yourself, and one thing you can do less of.
s: The sayings are ancient Delphic maxims discussed by Plutarch, not his own coinage.

# Moralia, On Having Many Friends
q: The pursuit of many friends prevents us
q: from making real friends at all.
c: Plutarch argues that true friendship requires time, attention and shared experience. If we spread ourselves too thin, we have many acquaintances and no friends. Better a few close friends than a crowd of half-friends.
p: Put extra effort into one close friendship today instead of many shallow ones.

# Moralia, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend
q: I don't need a friend who changes when I change and nods when I nod.
q: My shadow does that much better.
c: Plutarch warns against flatterers, who agree with everything to win favour. A real friend sometimes disagrees, tells you the truth and stays the same when you change for the worse.
p: Value one person today who disagrees with you. Tell them you appreciate their honesty.

# Moralia, On Listening to Lectures
q: Learn to listen, and you will profit even from those who speak badly.
c: Plutarch advises young listeners not to dismiss a speaker because of poor style. A good listener can find something worthwhile even in a weak lecture, by attending to the content, not the delivery.
p: In a conversation today with someone who bores you, look for one useful thing they say.

# Moralia, Political Precepts
q: It is not the office that honours the man,
q: but the man who honours the office.
c: Plutarch tells the story of the Theban general Epaminondas. His enemies, to humiliate him, made him supervisor of street cleaning. Epaminondas took the job seriously and turned it into a respected position. Plutarch himself held humble offices in his home town and was mocked for supervising the dumping of rubble.
p: Do a humble task today with the same seriousness you would bring to an important one.

# Moralia, On Superstition
q: The atheist thinks there are no gods;
q: the superstitious person wishes there were none, but believes against their will, out of fear.
c: Plutarch was a priest and believed in the gods. But he thought superstition was worse than atheism, because it made people live in constant fear of divine punishment. Real religion, he argued, should bring hope and joy.
p: Notice one fear today that is based on superstition or habit rather than reason. Let it go.

# Life of Sertorius 16
q: Perseverance is more powerful than violence.
q: Many things that cannot be overcome all at once yield when taken little by little.
c: To teach his soldiers patience, the Roman general Sertorius had two men try to pull out the tail of a horse. A strong man pulled at the whole tail at once and failed. A weak man pulled out the hairs one by one and succeeded. Plutarch tells the story and draws this lesson.
p: Break one big problem into small parts today and pull out one hair.

# Moralia, On Curiosity
q: Why do you look so sharply at the faults of others,
q: and overlook your own?
c: Plutarch's essay on curiosity targets the busybody who wants to know other people's secrets. He suggests turning that curiosity inward: there is plenty to investigate in our own lives, and the results are much more useful.
p: Every time you notice someone else's fault today, look for one of your own.

# Moralia, On Compliance
q: Those who cannot say no
q: are led into many shameful things.
c: Plutarch's essay addresses excessive shyness, the fear of disappointing people. He gives practical advice: practise saying no in small matters, so that you can do it in large ones. Being unable to refuse is not kindness but weakness.
p: Say no to one small request today that you would normally accept against your judgment.

# Life of Demosthenes 2
q: I live in a small city,
q: and I stay there willingly so that it does not become even smaller.
c: Plutarch explains why he chose to stay in his small hometown of Chaeronea, though he could have lived in Rome or Athens. It is a light remark, but it shows his loyalty to his community. He made it a centre of learning, and Chaeronea's fame today rests largely on him.
p: Do something today to make your own community a little bigger in spirit.

# Moralia, On Moral Virtue
q: Character is formed by habit.
q: The ethical virtues are produced by reason working on our emotions through practice.
c: Plutarch follows Aristotle in this view: character (ēthos) gets its name from habit (ethos). We are not simply born good or bad. Through repeated action guided by reason, our emotions are shaped and become virtues.
p: Repeat one good action today, consciously, as training.
