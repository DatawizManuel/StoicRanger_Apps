# Letters to Lucilius 1.1
q: Claim yourself for yourself.
q: Gather and guard the time that until now was taken from you, stolen or simply slipped away.
c: These are the first lines of the 124 letters Seneca wrote in the last years of his life to his friend Lucilius, a Roman official in Sicily. He opens not with theory but with time: most of it, he says, is taken by others, some is stolen, and the largest part simply drifts away while we are not paying attention.
c: "Hold every hour in your grasp," he continues. "You will depend less on tomorrow if you lay hold of today."
e: AD 62 · Rome | Seneca asks to retire | After his ally Burrus died, Seneca asked Nero for permission to withdraw from court and offered to hand over his vast fortune. Nero refused the money but Seneca withdrew anyway, avoided Rome and spent his time reading and writing. The letters to Lucilius were written in this hard-won free time.
p: Track where your hours go today. At the end of the day, mark which ones were taken, which were stolen, and which you actually chose.
s: Retirement: Tacitus, Annals 14.53–56.

# On the Shortness of Life 1.3
q: It is not that we have a short time to live,
q: but that we waste a great deal of it.
c: Seneca wrote this essay to Paulinus, an official in charge of Rome's grain supply, who was buried in work. Life is long enough, he argues, and generous enough for the greatest achievements, if the whole of it is well invested. We do not receive a short life; we make it short.
c: He lists the ways: chasing other people's approval, endless business, pointless busyness, idle pleasure. People guard their money carefully but give away their time to anyone who asks.
p: Find one thing today you do out of habit that you would not choose if you noticed it. Give that time to something you value.

# Letters to Lucilius 13.4
q: There are more things that frighten us than things that actually crush us.
q: We suffer more often in imagination than in reality.
c: Seneca writes to Lucilius about fear. Many of the things we dread never arrive; others are smaller than we pictured; others are still far off. Yet we suffer them all in advance. Before you give in to fear, he advises, ask whether the danger is certain, whether it is near, and whether it is really as bad as it looks.
p: Take your biggest worry today and ask three questions: is it certain? is it near? is it as bad as I imagine?

# Letters to Lucilius 7.8
q: People learn while they teach.
c: In this letter Seneca warns Lucilius against crowds, which bring vices home with them, and recommends spending time with people who can make him better, or whom he can make better. The help goes both ways. Explaining something to someone else is one of the best ways to understand it yourself.
p: Explain one idea you care about to someone today. Notice what you learn by trying.

# Letters to Lucilius 2.6
q: It is not the one who has too little who is poor,
q: but the one who wants more.
c: Seneca often borrowed lines from other philosophers, even rival schools, to close his letters. Here he makes a point the Stoics and Epicureans agreed on: poverty is not measured by what you have but by the gap between what you have and what you want. Close the gap and you are rich.
p: Before buying anything today that you don't need, wait. Ask whether you want the thing, or only the feeling of getting it.

# Letters to Lucilius 116.8
q: "I can't" is the excuse.
q: "I won't" is the reason.
c: Lucilius had suggested that some passions are too strong to resist. Seneca disagrees. We say we cannot because it is easier than admitting that we do not want to. We defend our faults, he says, because we love them, and we would rather excuse them than cure them.
p: Catch one "I can't" in your thoughts today. Honestly rename it "I won't" and see whether that changes your answer.

# Letters to Lucilius 28.1
q: You need to change your mind, not your sky.
c: Lucilius had travelled to escape his low mood and was surprised it came with him. Seneca replies with a remark of Socrates: why wonder that travel does not help you, when you always take yourself along? Wherever you go, your problems travel with you. A new place can refresh, but it cannot cure what lives in the mind.
p: If you are tempted to fix a bad mood by changing your surroundings today, first ask what thought you are carrying with you.

# On Anger 3.36
q: Every day the mind should be called to account.
q: What bad habit have you cured today? What fault have you resisted? In what way are you better?
c: Seneca describes the evening practice he learned from his teacher Quintus Sextius. After the lamp was put out and his wife had fallen silent, Seneca went through his whole day, examining what he had done and said, hiding nothing from himself. Then, he says, he slept peacefully.
e: c. 40 BC – AD 10 · Rome | The school of the Sextii | Quintus Sextius turned down a seat in the Senate offered by Julius Caesar to found a philosophical school in Rome. It combined Stoic ethics with Pythagorean habits like vegetarianism and nightly self-examination. It did not outlast its founders, but through Seneca, who studied with Sextius' followers, its evening review became one of the best-known Stoic exercises.
p: Tonight, before sleep, ask Sextius' three questions about your day. Be honest, and be kind to yourself in the answer.

# On Providence 5.10
q: Fire tests gold,
q: hardship tests the brave.
c: In On Providence Seneca answers the old question: why do bad things happen to good people? His answer is that hardship is a kind of training. Just as athletes need strong opponents, a person needs difficulty to show and grow their strength. The good are not spared trouble; they are tested by it.
e: AD 41–49 · Corsica | Eight years of exile | Accused of adultery with the emperor's niece, Seneca was banished to Corsica, a rough, malarial island he described with dislike. He was in his forties, cut off from his career and his family. Yet there he wrote consolations to his mother and to others, and deepened the philosophy that would shape his later works.
p: When something hard comes up today, think of it as the fire, not the end. What is it testing in you?

# Letters to Lucilius 18.5
q: Set aside some days on which you are content with very little and very plain food, with rough and coarse clothing,
q: and say to yourself: "Is this what I was afraid of?"
c: Seneca wrote this letter in December, during the Saturnalia, when Rome gave itself over to feasting. He did not recommend avoiding the party completely. But he did recommend practising poverty now and then, so that the fear of losing comfort loses its grip. Once you have lived with little, you discover how little you need.
e: December · Rome | The Saturnalia | The Saturnalia was Rome's winter festival in honour of Saturn, celebrated from 17 December. Shops closed, people exchanged gifts, gambling was allowed and slaves were served by their masters. Seneca wrote that "the whole city is in a sweat" and used the noise of the festival as the setting for his advice on simplicity.
p: Try one small voluntary discomfort today: a cold shower, a simple meal, a walk instead of a ride. Then ask: is this what I was afraid of?

# Letters to Lucilius 47.1
q: "They are slaves." No, they are human beings.
q: "They are slaves." No, they are housemates. "They are slaves." No, they are humble friends.
c: Seneca does not call for abolishing slavery, which almost no one in the ancient world did. But he insists that slaves are human beings, born of the same seed, under the same sky. He criticises masters who eat in silence while slaves stand hungry and are beaten for coughing. Treat those below you, he says, as you would want to be treated by those above you.
e: AD 61 · Rome | The slaves of Pedanius Secundus | When the city prefect Pedanius Secundus was murdered by one of his slaves, Roman law demanded that all the slaves in the household be executed, around four hundred people. Crowds protested, but the Senate voted for the old law and the sentence was carried out under military guard. It is the world in which Seneca's letter was written.
p: Notice how you treat someone today who serves you: a cashier, a waiter, a colleague below you. Treat them the way you would like to be treated.
s: Pedanius: Tacitus, Annals 14.42–45.

# Letters to Lucilius 91.4
q: Nothing should be unexpected by us.
q: We must send our minds ahead to meet everything, and consider not what usually happens but what can happen.
c: Seneca wrote this letter after hearing that a whole city had burned in a single night. A friend from that city was devastated. Seneca's advice is the Stoic exercise of premeditation: imagine in calm times the misfortunes that could come, so they do not take you by surprise.
e: AD 64 or 65 · Lugdunum (Lyon) | The fire of Lyon | Lugdunum, capital of Roman Gaul, burned to the ground in a single night. "There was one night between a great city and none," Seneca wrote. The city had just sent a large sum to Rome after its own great fire. Nero sent money in return to help rebuild.
p: Take one plan you are counting on and briefly imagine it failing. What would you do? Then go ahead, calmer.
s: Fire: Seneca, Letters 91; Tacitus, Annals 16.13.

# Letters to Lucilius 71.3
q: If you do not know which harbour you are heading for,
q: no wind is the right wind.
c: Lucilius had asked Seneca for advice on a specific decision. Seneca replies that individual decisions are hard to make without first knowing the goal of your whole life. Without that, every piece of advice is random. Once you know where you are going, decisions become easier.
p: Write one sentence describing where you are trying to go this year. Use it to make one decision today.

# On Anger 3.12
q: The greatest remedy for anger is delay.
c: Seneca's book On Anger, written for his brother Novatus, describes anger as a brief madness. Its first rush is the strongest. If you can give it time, it passes, and judgment returns. Seneca recommends asking anger for a delay, not for forgiveness: it does not need to be cancelled, just postponed.
p: When you feel anger rise today, give it a delay: ten breaths, ten minutes, or until tomorrow. Then decide.

# Letters to Lucilius 107.11
q: The fates lead the willing,
q: and drag the unwilling.
c: Seneca translates the prayer of Cleanthes into Latin verse and adds this final line, which became one of the most quoted sentences in Stoic writing. Events happen whether we agree or not. The only choice is whether to walk with them, or be dragged and complain.
p: Name one thing today that is going to happen whether you like it or not. Choose to walk with it.

# Letters to Lucilius 3.2
q: Think for a long time about whether to admit someone to your friendship.
q: But once you have decided, welcome them with all your heart, and speak with them as boldly as with yourself.
c: Lucilius had called someone a "friend" while saying he could not trust him with everything. Seneca objects. You should judge carefully before friendship, he says, and trust fully after. Many people do the reverse: trust quickly and then judge.
p: Think of one friend you trust fully. Tell them something you have been holding back.

# Letters to Lucilius 24.20
q: We die every day.
q: Every day a part of life is taken from us.
c: Death, Seneca argues, is not a single event ahead of us. It is a process that began at birth. Every hour that passes is already gone. The thought is not meant to depress but to change perspective: the last day does not bring death; it only completes it.
p: Look at today as a part of your life you are spending. What do you want to buy with it?

# On the Happy Life 26.1
q: The wise person does not love wealth, but prefers it.
q: They keep it in their house, not in their heart.
c: Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome, and critics called him a hypocrite. In On the Happy Life he answers them. A philosopher need not be poor. What matters is whether you can lose your wealth without losing yourself. If it leaves, it takes only itself with it.
e: AD 58 · Rome | The attack of Suillius | Publius Suillius Rufus, a prosecutor under the previous emperor, attacked Seneca in public: what philosophy, he asked, had allowed Seneca to collect 300 million sesterces in four years of royal friendship? Suillius was exiled, but the accusation stuck, and Seneca defended his position in writing.
p: Name one thing you own that you would struggle to lose. Imagine losing it, and remind yourself you would still be you.
s: Suillius: Tacitus, Annals 13.42.

# On the Shortness of Life 7.3
q: It takes a whole life to learn how to live,
q: and, what may surprise you more, it takes a whole life to learn how to die.
c: Seneca observes that many skills can be learned quickly, even by the young. But living well cannot be mastered once and filed away; it is a lifelong study. Many of the greatest people, he says, gave up wealth, pleasure and duties so they could focus on this single thing until the end.
p: What is one thing about living well you are still learning? Practise it once today.

# Letters to Lucilius 24.13
q: Not only from people, but from things too, the mask must be removed,
q: and their true face restored.
c: Seneca describes how children are frightened by masks worn by people they love. Adults are no different. Swords, fire, prison, pain and death look terrible when dressed up by fear. Look at them plainly, he says, and see what they really are: often less than their reputation.
p: Name something you dread. Take off its mask by describing it in plain words.

# Letters to Lucilius 33.11
q: Those who went before us are not our masters
q: but our guides.
c: Lucilius asked for more quotations from great philosophers. Seneca warns him against simply collecting famous lines. The truth is open to all and has not yet been fully discovered. Use the ancients as guides, not as masters to be blindly followed, and add something of your own.
p: Take one rule or saying you follow without thinking, and ask whether you agree with it after all.

# Letters to Lucilius 95.52
q: We are limbs of one great body.
c: Seneca argues that all human beings are bound together by nature. Born from the same source and for the same purpose, we owe each other help. Like stones in an arch, he adds, we would fall if we did not hold each other up.
p: Do one small thing today for someone who has no way to repay you.

# Letters to Lucilius 101.10
q: Begin to live at once,
q: and count each separate day as a separate life.
c: Seneca writes after the sudden death of a friend, Senecio, who had been busy making plans for the future. We keep postponing life, he notes, waiting for the next thing. Instead, treat each day as complete in itself.
p: Live today as a whole life: start something, do something well, end the day in peace.

# Letters to Lucilius 56.5
q: There may be uproar outside,
q: as long as there is none within.
c: Seneca wrote this letter while living above a public bath. He describes the noise in detail: weightlifters grunting, masseurs slapping, a man who loved the sound of his own voice in the bath, sellers of cakes and sausages. His point is that true quiet does not come from silence outside, but from a mind at rest.
e: c. AD 63 · Seneca's lodgings | Above the bathhouse | Roman public baths were loud, crowded social centres where people exercised, bathed, ate and gossiped. Seneca, rich enough to live anywhere, found himself in rooms directly above one and used it as a test of his calm, listing every noise with obvious amusement.
p: Next time noise distracts you today, treat it as training: return your attention to your task without blaming the noise.

# Tacitus, Annals 15.62
q: Since I am not allowed to repay your services,
q: I leave you the one thing I still have, and the finest: the pattern of my life.
c: When the order came from Nero to die, Seneca asked for writing tablets to make his will. The centurion refused. Seneca turned to his friends and spoke these words, as Tacitus reports. If they remembered it, he said, they would win a reputation for virtue and loyal friendship.
e: April AD 65 · Near Rome | The death of Seneca | After the Pisonian conspiracy against Nero was discovered, Seneca was ordered to take his own life. He opened his veins, but the blood flowed slowly; he took poison, which failed; and he was finally placed in a warm bath, where he died. He dictated his last thoughts to secretaries until the end.
p: What would someone learn from the pattern of your life today, if they only saw that?
s: Tacitus, Annals 15.60–64. Seneca's last words reported by Tacitus.
