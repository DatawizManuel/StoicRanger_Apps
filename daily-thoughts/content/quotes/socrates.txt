# Plato, Apology 38a
q: The unexamined life
q: is not worth living for a human being.
c: Socrates said this at his trial, after being found guilty. He had been asked to propose a penalty, and someone suggested he could go into exile and keep quiet. He refused. To stop questioning himself and others, he said, would be to stop living as a human being at all.
e: 399 BC · The People's Court, Athens | The trial of Socrates | Socrates, then seventy, was accused by Meletus, Anytus and Lycon of not recognising the city's gods, introducing new ones and corrupting the young. A jury of about five hundred citizens heard the case in a single day. He was found guilty by a margin of around sixty votes and sentenced to death.
p: Set aside ten minutes today to examine one thing you do automatically. Why do you do it?

# Plato, Apology 21d
q: I seem to be wiser than this man in just this small way:
q: that what I do not know, I do not think I know.
c: Socrates explains how he came to question people. After the oracle said no one was wiser than he, he went to a politician with a reputation for wisdom and found that the man knew very little, but thought he knew a great deal. Socrates did not know either, but at least he was aware of it.
e: c. 430 BC · Delphi | The oracle | Socrates' friend Chaerephon, an impulsive man, went to the oracle of Apollo at Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The priestess answered: no one. Socrates, puzzled, set out to prove the oracle wrong and ended by understanding what it meant.
p: In one conversation today, say "I don't know" when you don't. Notice how it opens the conversation.

# Plato, Apology 30b
q: Virtue does not come from money,
q: but from virtue come money and all other good things for human beings, both private and public.
c: Socrates tells the jury what he has been doing for years: going around Athens urging young and old not to care for their bodies or money before caring for the state of their souls. Character comes first; everything else depends on it.
p: Before one decision today, ask which option makes you a better person, not only a richer or more comfortable one.

# Plato, Apology 41d
q: No evil can happen to a good person,
q: either in life or after death.
c: These words come from Socrates' final speech to the jurors who had voted for his acquittal. He tells them to be of good hope about death. Whatever happens to the body, the good person's character cannot be harmed by others. That is the only thing truly theirs.
p: When you fear losing something today, ask: can this loss make me worse, or only make things harder?

# Plato, Apology 29d
q: Men of Athens, I respect and love you, but I will obey the god rather than you.
q: As long as I draw breath and am able, I will never stop doing philosophy.
c: Socrates imagines the jury offering to let him go if he promises to stop questioning people. He refuses. He sees his questioning as a duty given to him, and he would rather die than abandon it.
p: Name one thing you would not give up even under pressure. Live by it today.

# Plato, Apology 30e
q: The god has attached me to the city like a gadfly to a large, well-bred horse
q: that is sluggish because of its size and needs to be stirred up.
c: Socrates compares himself to an insect that stings a lazy horse. Athens is great but complacent; he is the irritating presence that keeps it awake. If they kill him, he warns, they will not easily find another.
p: Is there someone whose criticism annoys you but keeps you sharp? Thank them, at least in your mind.

# Plato, Apology 42a
q: Now it is time to go: I to die, and you to live.
q: Which of us goes to the better thing is unclear to everyone except the god.
c: These are the last words of Socrates' speech at his trial. He ends without bitterness, with the calm of someone who has lived by his principles and accepts the consequences.
p: End your day today without bitterness. Whatever happened, let it go before you sleep.

# Plato, Crito 49b
q: We must never do wrong,
q: nor, when wronged, do wrong in return, as most people think.
c: In prison, his friend Crito urges Socrates to escape, since he was condemned unjustly. Socrates refuses. Escaping would mean breaking the laws, and doing wrong in return for wrong is still doing wrong. This was a radical idea in a culture where taking revenge was considered honourable.
e: 399 BC · Prison, Athens | The escape plan | Because a sacred ship had gone to Delos and no executions could take place until it returned, Socrates spent about a month in prison. His friends prepared an escape, with money ready to bribe the guards and a safe home in Thessaly. Socrates refused to leave.
p: If someone wrongs you today, refuse to wrong them back, in deed or in words.

# Plato, Crito 48b
q: What we should value most
q: is not living, but living well.
c: Socrates reminds Crito of a principle they had agreed on many times before. The length of a life is not what matters most; its quality is. A short life lived well is better than a long life lived badly.
p: Do one thing today that makes this day well lived, not just lived.

# Plato, Phaedo 118a
q: Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius.
q: Pay it, and do not forget.
c: These were Socrates' last words, spoken after he had drunk the hemlock. Asclepius was the god of healing, and a cock was offered to him by those who had recovered from illness. Some readers think Socrates meant that death was a cure for the illness of life; others that it was a simple act of piety. Either way, he died thinking of a debt to be paid.
e: 399 BC · Prison, Athens | The death of Socrates | On his last day, Socrates spent hours discussing the immortality of the soul with his friends. He bathed, said goodbye to his family, and drank the hemlock calmly. When his friends wept, he scolded them gently: he had sent the women away to avoid such scenes. He walked about until his legs felt heavy, then lay down.
p: Is there a small debt or promise you have forgotten? Settle it today.

# Plato, Phaedo 67e
q: Those who practise philosophy rightly
q: are practising for dying.
c: On his last day, Socrates explains to his friends why he is not afraid. Philosophy, he says, trains the mind to free itself from the demands of the body. A life spent loosening those bonds is a preparation for death, so the philosopher should not fear it when it comes.
p: Loosen your grip on one bodily comfort today, and notice that you remain yourself.

# Plato, Gorgias 469c
q: If I had to choose between doing wrong and suffering wrong,
q: I would choose to suffer it rather than to do it.
c: In the Gorgias, the young Polus is astonished: surely the tyrant who can do whatever he likes is happy? Socrates replies that doing injustice is the greatest harm, because it damages the soul. Being wronged only damages what is outside us.
e: 406 BC · The Assembly, Athens | The trial of the generals | After the naval victory at Arginusae, a storm prevented the rescue of shipwrecked sailors, and the Assembly wanted to try the eight commanders together and illegally. Socrates, by lot presiding officer that day, refused to put the motion to a vote, despite threats of arrest. The generals were condemned anyway, and the Athenians later regretted it.
p: If you face a choice today between doing something wrong and accepting a loss, take the loss.
s: Arginusae: Plato, Apology 32b; Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7.

# Plato, Theaetetus 155d
q: This feeling of wonder is the mark of a philosopher.
q: Philosophy has no other beginning.
c: Socrates says this to the young mathematician Theaetetus, who has confessed that some puzzles make him dizzy. Socrates is delighted. Being puzzled, he says, is not a sign of failure but the start of all real thinking.
p: Let something puzzle you today. Instead of looking up the answer straight away, wonder about it.

# Plato, Phaedrus 279b
q: Dear Pan and all you other gods of this place, grant that I may become beautiful within,
q: and that all I have outside may be in harmony with what is inside.
c: At the end of the Phaedrus, Socrates offers this prayer by a river outside Athens. He asks not for wealth or success but for inner beauty, and for his outer life to match it. He goes on to ask that he may consider the wise person rich, and have only as much gold as a self-controlled person could carry.
p: Ask yourself today where your outer life and inner values do not match. Bring one small thing into harmony.

# Xenophon, Memorabilia 1.6.10
q: To need nothing is divine;
q: to need as little as possible is nearest to the divine.
c: Socrates said this to the sophist Antiphon, who mocked him for his poor clothes, bare feet and simple food. Antiphon thought happiness lay in luxury. Socrates replied that the fewer needs we have, the freer we are, and the closer we come to the gods, who need nothing.
p: Do without one thing today that you usually think you need.

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives 2.25
q: How many things I can do without!
c: Socrates often walked through the Athenian market, where all kinds of goods were displayed for sale. Instead of wishing for them, he would say this to himself. The market became a lesson in freedom rather than desire.
p: Next time you shop or scroll through things to buy, say to yourself: "How many things I can do without."

# Plato, Apology 28e
q: Wherever someone has taken up their post, believing it best, or been placed there by their commander,
q: there, I think, they must remain and face the danger, without counting the cost in death or anything else, rather than disgrace.
c: Socrates reminds the jury that he served as a soldier and held his post when ordered. He will hold the post his conscience has given him in the same way, even at the cost of his life.
e: 432 and 424 BC · Potidaea and Delium | Socrates the soldier | At the siege of Potidaea, Socrates saved the wounded Alcibiades and gave the prize for bravery to him. In the retreat after the Athenian defeat at Delium, he walked calmly through the chaos, looking around so steadily that no enemy dared to attack him or those near him. Alcibiades later told both stories with admiration.
p: Where is your post today, at work, at home, among friends? Hold it well, even if it gets uncomfortable.
s: Military stories: Plato, Symposium 219e–221c.

# Plato, Apology 32d
q: I showed, not in words but in deeds, that I did not care about death in the least.
q: My whole concern was to do nothing unjust or unholy.
c: Socrates describes how he refused an order from the Thirty, the oligarchs who seized power in Athens. He did not argue; he simply went home. He would probably have been killed if the regime had not fallen soon afterwards.
e: 404 BC · Athens | The arrest of Leon of Salamis | The Thirty Tyrants tried to implicate as many citizens as possible in their crimes. They ordered Socrates and four other men to arrest Leon of Salamis, an innocent man, so he could be executed. The other four went. Socrates went home. Leon was killed anyway.
p: If you are asked today to go along with something wrong, quietly do not.

# Cicero, On Ends 2.90
q: Hunger is the best sauce.
c: Cicero reports that Socrates was once seen walking hard until evening. When asked why, he said he was building up an appetite for dinner. Simple food tastes best when we are truly hungry; the best pleasures often come from need, not from luxury.
p: Let yourself get properly hungry before your next meal, and notice how good simple food tastes.

# Xenophon, Symposium 2.10
q: People who want to become good horsemen choose the most spirited horses.
q: I wanted to live among people, so I married her, knowing that if I could get along with her, I could get along with anyone.
c: At a dinner party, a friend asked Socrates why he had married Xanthippe, known as the most difficult woman in Athens. Socrates answered with this joke, comparing marriage to training. It is unfair to Xanthippe, but it shows Socrates' view of difficulty: a hard companion is a teacher.
p: With one difficult person today, think of yourself as in training. What are they teaching you?
