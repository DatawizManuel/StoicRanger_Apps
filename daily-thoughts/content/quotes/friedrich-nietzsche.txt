# The Gay Science 276
q: I want to learn more and more to see what is necessary in things as beautiful.
q: Then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. Amor fati: let that be my love from now on.
c: Nietzsche wrote this at the start of Book 4 of The Gay Science, as a New Year's resolution for 1882. He adds that he does not want to wage war against what is ugly, or accuse, or even accuse the accusers. "Looking away shall be my only negation." It is his version of the Stoic acceptance of fate, but warmer: not only to bear what happens, but to love it.
p: Find one thing today that you cannot change, and look for what is beautiful in it.

# Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Clever" 10
q: My formula for greatness in a human being is amor fati:
q: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity.
c: In Ecce Homo, his strange autobiography written just before his breakdown, Nietzsche goes further than the Stoics. Not merely to endure what is necessary, still less to hide from it, he says, but to love it.
p: Look at one painful part of your past. Can you see how it helped make you who you are?

# Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Arrows" 8
q: From life's school of war:
q: what does not kill me makes me stronger.
c: This is one of the most quoted lines Nietzsche ever wrote, often used carelessly. For him it was personal. He lived with constant migraines, near-blindness and stomach illness, and turned the suffering into a discipline for his thinking.
e: 1870 · Franco-Prussian War | The medical orderly | A Swiss citizen and Basel professor, Nietzsche volunteered as a medical orderly in the Prussian army. He spent days in a cattle car caring for wounded soldiers and contracted dysentery and diphtheria. His health never fully recovered, and the rest of his life was shaped by illness.
p: Name one hardship that left you stronger. Remember it the next time something hard comes.

# Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Arrows" 12
q: If we have our own why of life,
q: we can bear almost any how.
c: Nietzsche says that a person with a strong reason for living can endure almost any circumstance. Meaning makes hardship bearable. Without it, even comfort becomes unbearable.
e: 1942–1945 · Nazi concentration camps | Frankl's why | The Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl survived Theresienstadt, Auschwitz and Dachau. In his book Man's Search for Meaning (1946), he quoted Nietzsche's line again and again: prisoners who still had a reason to live, a person waiting for them, a work to finish, were more likely to survive. The line became the motto of his logotherapy.
p: Write down your own "why" in one sentence. Read it when the "how" gets hard today.

# The Gay Science 290
q: One thing is needful: to give style to one's character.
q: A great and rare art.
c: Nietzsche describes how a person can survey all the strengths and weaknesses of their nature and shape them into an artistic plan: adding a piece here, removing one there, until even the weaknesses delight the eye. Character is not simply given; it can be crafted, like a work of art.
p: Pick one trait you don't like in yourself. How could you shape it into something that fits the whole?

# The Gay Science 283
q: The secret for harvesting the greatest fruitfulness and greatest enjoyment from existence is: live dangerously!
q: Build your cities on the slopes of Vesuvius! Send your ships into uncharted seas!
c: Nietzsche writes for "more courageous" people who seek knowledge as an adventure. The comfortable life, he thinks, makes people small. Growth requires risk. It is not a call to recklessness but to courage.
p: Take one small, deliberate risk today that could help you grow.

# The Gay Science 341
q: What if some day or night a demon were to steal into your loneliest loneliness and say to you:
q: "This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more."
c: Nietzsche introduces his thought of the eternal recurrence. Would you curse the demon, or call him a god? The test is simple and terrifying: live in such a way that you could will every moment to return forever.
e: August 1881 · Sils Maria, Switzerland | The rock at Surlej | Walking beside Lake Silvaplana in the Upper Engadine, Nietzsche stopped by a huge pyramid-shaped rock near Surlej. There, he later wrote, the thought of eternal recurrence came to him, "6,000 feet beyond man and time". He spent seven summers in Sils Maria; his rented room is now a museum.
p: Live one hour today in a way you would be happy to repeat forever.

# Beyond Good and Evil 146
q: Whoever fights monsters should see to it that in the process they do not become a monster.
q: And if you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss also gazes into you.
c: Nietzsche warns that the struggle against something bad can change us into what we fight. Constant attention to darkness can darken us. Marcus Aurelius said the same in a different way: the best revenge is not to become like the wrongdoer.
p: In a struggle today, check that you are not becoming what you oppose.

# Beyond Good and Evil 9
q: You want to live "according to nature"? O you noble Stoics, what deceptive words!
q: Imagine a being like nature, wasteful beyond measure, indifferent beyond measure, without purpose or consideration.
c: Nietzsche mocks the Stoic formula. Nature, he says, is not rational or kind; the Stoics read their own ideals into it and then claimed to follow it. It is a sharp challenge to the Stoics, and a useful one: are we following nature, or our own picture of it?
p: Examine one "natural" assumption you live by. Is it really nature, or a story you've told yourself?

# Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Prologue 5
q: I say unto you: one must still have chaos in oneself
q: to be able to give birth to a dancing star.
c: Zarathustra speaks to a crowd in the marketplace. He warns that a time will come when people will no longer have chaos in them, and so will create nothing great. Inner turmoil, rightly used, is the raw material of creation.
p: Instead of fighting some inner restlessness today, ask what it might help you create.

# Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Prologue 4
q: Man is a rope, stretched between animal and overman:
q: a rope over an abyss.
c: Nietzsche's Zarathustra sees humanity not as a finished product but as a bridge, something in transition. What is great in a human being is that they are a crossing, not a destination.
p: Ask yourself today what you are crossing toward.

# Thus Spoke Zarathustra, "On Reading and Writing"
q: Of all that is written I love only what a person has written with their blood.
q: Write with blood, and you will learn that blood is spirit.
c: Nietzsche despised writing that was merely clever or correct. He valued writing that came from real experience and cost the writer something. The Meditations of Marcus Aurelius would have passed his test.
p: Write a few honest lines today about something you have really lived.

# Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Arrows" 33
q: Without music
q: life would be a mistake.
c: Nietzsche was a passionate musician and composer, who played the piano daily and wrote songs and piano works. Music, for him, was the art closest to life itself. His friendship and later break with Richard Wagner shaped his whole philosophy.
e: 1869–1872 · Tribschen, Lake Lucerne | Visits to Wagner | As a young professor in Basel, Nietzsche visited Richard Wagner and Cosima von Bülow at their villa at Tribschen more than twenty times. He was present when the Siegfried Idyll was first played on the staircase on Christmas morning 1870. He later called those days the best of his life, even after his bitter break with Wagner.
p: Listen to one piece of music today with full attention, doing nothing else.

# Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Arrows" 34
q: Only thoughts reached by walking
q: have any value.
c: Nietzsche walked for hours every day, often six to eight hours, with a notebook. He wrote most of his books from notes taken on these walks in the mountains and along the coast. He distrusted ideas that came from sitting still.
p: Take a walk today without your phone, and see what thoughts come.

# The Gay Science 270
q: What does your conscience say?
q: "You shall become who you are."
c: This short aphorism echoes the ancient Greek poet Pindar, and Nietzsche later used it as the subtitle of Ecce Homo: "How One Becomes What One Is". The task of life is not to become someone else, but to grow into the person you already are in potential.
p: Do one thing today that is truly you, not what others expect.

# Human, All Too Human I, 483
q: Convictions are more dangerous enemies of truth
q: than lies.
c: A liar knows he is lying. A person with fixed convictions has stopped looking. Nietzsche argues that strong beliefs can block inquiry more effectively than deliberate falsehood.
p: Hold one of your convictions more loosely today. Look for evidence against it.

# Daybreak 573
q: The snake that cannot shed its skin perishes.
q: So too with minds that are prevented from changing their opinions: they cease to be minds.
c: Nietzsche believed that growth requires letting go of old ideas. A mind that never changes is dead. Being willing to revise one's views is not weakness but life.
p: Change your mind about one small thing today, if the evidence asks you to.

# The Antichrist 55
q: The most common lie
q: is the one we tell ourselves.
c: Nietzsche notes that lying to others is rare compared with lying to oneself: not wanting to see something as it is. Self-deception is the foundation on which other deceptions are built. Marcus Aurelius made the same point when he wrote that he sought the truth, by which no one was ever harmed.
p: Name one thing you have been avoiding seeing clearly about yourself.

# Schopenhauer as Educator 1
q: No one can build you the bridge on which you, and only you, must cross the river of life.
q: There may be countless paths and bridges and demigods ready to carry you across, but only at the price of yourself.
c: In this early essay Nietzsche urges young people to stop following others and find their own path. Teachers can show the way, but no one can walk it for you.
p: Take one step today on a path only you can walk.

# Thus Spoke Zarathustra, "On the Spirit of Gravity"
q: "This is my way; where is yours?"
q: So I answered those who asked me "the way". For the way does not exist.
c: Zarathustra refuses to give his followers a single correct path. Each person must find their own. This is the opposite of a rule book: it is an invitation to self-responsibility.
p: Instead of asking someone what to do today, decide your own way.

# Twilight of the Idols, "Maxims and Arrows" 26
q: I distrust all systematisers and avoid them.
q: The will to a system is a lack of integrity.
c: Nietzsche distrusted philosophers who forced everything into a tidy system. Reality is too complex, he thought, and the urge to make it fit shows that the thinker cares more about order than about truth. That is why he wrote in aphorisms.
p: Notice one place where you are forcing a messy reality into a neat explanation. Let it stay messy.

# On the Use and Abuse of History for Life 1
q: Consider the cattle, grazing as they pass you by: they do not know what yesterday or today is.
q: They leap about, eat, rest, digest and leap again, from morning to night, from day to day.
c: Nietzsche envies the animal's freedom from memory. Humans carry the past as a weight, and too much of it can crush life. A healthy person, he argues, knows how to forget as well as to remember.
p: Let go of one memory today that is weighing on you without teaching you anything.

# Thus Spoke Zarathustra, "On Self-Overcoming"
q: And life itself told me this secret:
q: "Behold," it said, "I am that which must always overcome itself."
c: For Nietzsche, life is not a fixed state but continual growth. Everything alive pushes beyond itself. The best in a human being is their ability to overcome what they were.
p: Overcome one small habit or fear today.

# The Gay Science 125
q: God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him.
q: How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?
c: These words are spoken by a madman who runs into the marketplace with a lantern in broad daylight. The crowd laughs at him. Nietzsche is not celebrating; he is describing a crisis: if the old foundations of meaning are gone, what will people live by? His answer was that we must find or create values ourselves.
p: Ask yourself today what your values really rest on.

# Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Wise" 6
q: Freedom from resentment, understanding resentment:
q: who knows how much I ultimately owe to my long illness for this too!
c: Nietzsche writes that resentment, the poisonous brooding over injuries, is the most harmful reaction of all, especially for someone weak or ill. He learned to avoid it as a kind of self-preservation, accepting even what was hard, like a Russian soldier who lies down in the snow without fighting it.
e: 3 January 1889 · Turin | The collapse | Nietzsche suffered a complete mental breakdown in Turin. A famous but unverified story says it began when he threw his arms around a horse being beaten in the Piazza Carlo Alberto. He sent strange letters to friends, was brought back to Basel by his friend Franz Overbeck, and never recovered. He died in Weimar in 1900.
p: Let go of one resentment today, for your own sake.
